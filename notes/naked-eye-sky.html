The Naked-Eye Sky

What you're actually looking at in the night sky with no telescope or binoculars: how to tell one kind of light from another, and the star patterns worth learning first. How to observe is covered on its companion page, Observing Techniques.

What's up there: naked-eye phenomena

The Sun

By far the brightest object in the sky. Never look at it directly, even briefly, and never through binoculars or a telescope. See Observing the Sun for safe methods.

The Moon

The brightest object at night. It goes through a cycle of phases every ~29.5 days, and even without a telescope you can pick out its dark maria and bright highlands.

Stars

Sharp points of light that , especially near the horizon. They keep the same patterns night after night and year after year. The brightest show colour: compare orange-red Betelgeuse with blue-white Rigel in Orion. Some are — two stars that appear close together, sometimes genuinely orbiting one another — which is a different idea entirely from a constellation or asterism, just an unrelated group of stars that happen to line up as seen from Earth. A truly dark site shows a few thousand stars at once; a city centre, only a few dozen.

Star clusters

A group of stars born together, still close enough to look like a tight knot or a fuzzy patch, rather than the loose, coincidental pattern of a constellation or asterism. The best naked-eye example is the Pleiades (the Seven Sisters), in Taurus: most people can pick out five or six stars unaided, more from a dark site.

Galaxies and nebulae

Two different things that can each look like a faint smudge. A galaxy is an entire separate star system, like our own but so far away its billions of stars blur into one glow — the Andromeda Galaxy is the only one easily seen with the naked eye from the UK. A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust much closer, inside our own Galaxy, sometimes lit up by young stars nearby, like the Orion Nebula below Orion's Belt.

Planets

Five are easy to see: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. They look like bright stars but shine with a steadier light, stay within the zodiacal band, and drift slowly against the background stars over days and weeks. Venus, the brightest, is only ever seen in the evening or morning sky, never far from the Sun.

Comets

A fuzzy patch, the coma, often with a faint tail that always points away from the Sun. A comet drifts slowly against the stars from one night to the next; it does not streak across the sky. Bright ones only come along every few years.

Meteors

"Shooting stars": a brief streak of light, usually lasting under a second, as a grain of dust burns up high in the atmosphere. A few an hour appear on any dark night, and many more during a meteor shower.

Aurorae

Glowing curtains, arcs or rays of light, mostly green (sometimes red or purple), that can shift and ripple over minutes. They're caused by solar wind particles, guided by Earth's magnetic field, striking gas high in the atmosphere. They're seen most often near the poles; from the UK, usually low in the north, and mainly during strong solar activity.

Supernovae

A dying star's catastrophic explosion, briefly outshining every other star in its galaxy. One bright enough to see unaided in our own Galaxy is extremely rare — the last was in 1604. Supernovae are found often in other galaxies, but only ever through a telescope: at that distance, they're far too faint for the naked eye.

Artificial satellites

A star-like point that moves steadily in a straight line, crossing the sky in a few minutes, with no flashing lights — unlike an aircraft. They shine by reflected sunlight, so they're best seen in the hours after sunset or before dawn, and may fade out mid-sky as they pass into Earth's shadow. The International Space Station is the brightest.

Aircraft

Not an astronomical object at all, but the thing most often mistaken for one. Unlike a satellite, an aircraft blinks — steady or flashing red, green and white lights — and often shows colour, so that's the giveaway: satellites glide on steadily with no blinking lights; aircraft blink and often show colour.

The Milky Way

On a dark, moonless night the Milky Way appears as a faint, diffuse band of light arching right across the sky. It's the combined glow of billions of stars too distant and faint to see one by one. Our Galaxy is a flat disc, and we live inside it, so when we look along the disc we see those stars piled up into a band. Dark patches and lanes along it, such as the Great Rift through Cygnus, are clouds of dust blocking the light of the stars behind them.

Its glow is so faint that even moderate light pollution hides it completely, so you need a genuinely dark site, well away from towns, with your eyes fully dark-adapted. See Light pollution and skyglow and dark adaptation on the Observing Techniques page. From the UK it's at its best on late summer and autumn evenings, running high overhead through Cygnus and Cassiopeia.

Constellations and asterisms

A is one of the 88 official regions the sky is divided into, each containing a traditional star pattern. An is any other recognisable pattern of stars: often part of a constellation, like the Plough, or made of stars from several, like the Summer Triangle. Of the seven below, Cassiopeia, Orion, Cygnus and the Southern Cross are official constellations; the Plough, the Summer Triangle and the Square of Pegasus are asterisms — the Plough is part of the constellation Ursa Major, the Square is part of Pegasus and Andromeda, and the Summer Triangle spans three separate constellations altogether. They're grouped and shaded below by which is which, and each card names its own kind too.

Each diagram is drawn from the stars' real positions, like a star chart: north at the top and east on the left. Bigger dots are brighter stars. In the real sky the patterns keep their shapes but turn as the sky rotates through the night.

Cassiopeia

Five stars in a distinctive W (or M, depending on which way up it is). Circumpolar from the UK, it sits on the opposite side of Polaris from the Plough, so when one is low the other is high. Best high overhead on autumn and winter evenings.

Orion

The Hunter. Look for three stars in a short, straight row: his Belt. Orange-red Betelgeuse marks a shoulder, and blue-white Rigel a knee. The fuzzy patch below the Belt is the Orion Nebula. From the UK it's the centrepiece of winter evenings, in the south, and the Belt is itself a pointer to fainter objects (see below).

Cygnus

The Swan, flying down the Milky Way. Its brightest stars form a large cross, often called the Northern Cross (an asterism in its own right), with Deneb at the top and Albireo at the foot. High overhead on summer and autumn evenings from the UK.

The Southern Cross

Crux, the smallest of all 88 constellations: a compact, kite-shaped cross of bright stars in the Milky Way. It's too far south to see from the UK. To see the whole cross you need to be south of about , so it's a southern-hemisphere sight.

The Plough

A "saucepan" of seven stars: the brightest part of the constellation Ursa Major, the Great Bear. From the UK it's , so it's up on every clear night: high overhead on spring evenings, low in the north in autumn. Merak and Dubhe are the pointers to Polaris (see below).

The Summer Triangle

A big triangle made from the brightest stars of three different constellations: Vega in Lyra, Deneb in Cygnus and Altair in Aquila. High in the south on summer and early autumn evenings, with the Milky Way running through it.

The Square of Pegasus

A large, fairly empty square of four similar stars, high in the south on autumn evenings. Three of its corners belong to Pegasus, the Winged Horse. The fourth, Alpheratz, is officially part of the neighbouring constellation Andromeda. It's also a pointer to fainter objects in its own right (see below).

Using pointer stars

Two stars in a pattern, followed on in a straight line, will often land you on something else worth knowing — from due north, to a star too faint to spot any other way. Three pairs of pointers are worth learning: the Plough's, for north; and Orion's Belt's and the Square of Pegasus's, for four more naked-eye objects.

Finding north: the Plough and Polaris

Polaris, the Pole Star, lies within a degree of the north celestial pole, so it barely moves all night: it always sits almost due north, at an altitude roughly equal to your latitude. It isn't especially bright, though, so it's found with pointer stars:

  1. Find the Plough.
  2. Take the two stars at the end of its bowl, Merak then Dubhe: the Pointers.
  3. Follow the line from Merak through Dubhe for about times the gap between them.
  4. The moderately bright star you land on is Polaris. Straight down from it to the horizon is due north.

However the Plough is turned, the Pointers always point at Polaris, because the whole sky turns around that point. Move the slider to see the evening sky through the year.

Drawn from the real star positions for latitude 52°N (central England), at 10 pm GMT in the middle of each month (11 pm during British Summer Time). The Plough and Cassiopeia never dip below the horizon from here: that's what circumpolar means.

Once you've found Polaris, you can use its altitude to work out your latitude.

In the Southern Hemisphere there's no bright pole star. Instead, follow the long axis of the Southern Cross, from Gacrux through Acrux, for about times its length, to reach a point close to the south celestial pole.

From Orion's Belt: Sirius, Aldebaran and the Pleiades

Orion's three-star Belt is itself a pointer, in both directions. Follow it one way and it leads to the brightest star in the whole sky; the other way, to a bright orange star and then on to a small, unmistakable cluster.

  1. Find Orion's Belt: three stars in a short, straight row, Mintaka, Alnilam and Alnitak.
  2. Follow the line down and to the left (away from Mintaka) for about times the Mintaka–Alnilam gap, to reach Sirius — the brightest star in the night sky.
  3. Follow the same line the other way, up and to the right (away from Alnilam), for about times the gap, to reach orange Aldebaran, the eye of Taurus the Bull.
  4. Carry on a little further along the same line, about times the gap in total, to reach the Pleiades (the Seven Sisters): a small, tight knot of stars, a star cluster rather than a single star.

Drawn from the real star positions. The Pleiades is shown as the small cluster it actually is, not a single star.

From the Square of Pegasus: Fomalhaut and the Andromeda Galaxy

The Square points two different ways to two very different targets: a lone bright star low in the south, and the most distant thing anyone can see without a telescope.

  1. Find the Square of Pegasus.
  2. Follow its western side, from Scheat through Markab, for about times their gap, continuing down towards the southern horizon, to reach Fomalhaut — often called "the loneliest star", since there's little else bright nearby.
  3. Follow the diagonal instead, from Markab through Alpheratz, for about times its length, to reach a faint, elongated smudge: the Andromeda Galaxy, over two million light years away and the most distant object visible to the naked eye.

Fomalhaut is too far south to fit this diagram to scale: the arrow shows the right direction, not the true distance. The Andromeda Galaxy is close enough to the Square to be drawn to scale.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: comets and meteors. A meteor is a tiny grain burning up in our atmosphere, gone in a second. A comet is a large icy body millions of kilometres away, visible for weeks and moving only slowly against the stars. Meteor showers happen when Earth passes through the dust a comet has left behind. Common exam confusion: twinkling is caused by Earth's atmosphere, not by the star itself. Planets twinkle less because they show a tiny disc rather than a single point, so the atmosphere's wobbles average out. Naked-eye phenomena ↑

Common exam confusion: the Plough is not a constellation. It's an asterism, part of the constellation Ursa Major. The Summer Triangle and the Square of Pegasus are asterisms too, each using stars from more than one constellation. Constellations and asterisms ↑
