Catalogues and star names

One star can have several names. Each one tells you something about it.

One star, four names

Tap a star in Orion to see all its names, or choose which kind of name to label every star with.

  • Proper name: an old name, most from Arabic, such as Betelgeuse. Only brighter stars have one.
  • Bayer name: a Greek letter plus the constellation, such as Alpha Orionis. More below.
  • Flamsteed number: a number plus the constellation, such as 58 Orionis, numbered west to east.
  • Catalogue number: the star's entry in a big catalogue, such as HD 39801 in the Henry Draper (HD) Catalogue, which lists about 225,000 stars.

These names, the numbers and that most proper names come from Arabic: first draft, to be checked by the teacher. Positions and magnitudes are the same as on The Naked-Eye Sky.

Reading a Bayer name

In the Bayer system, a star's name is a Greek letter followed by the constellation's name in its Latin “of” form, the . The brightest star is usually Alpha (α), the next Beta (β), then Gamma (γ), and so on. So Beta Persei (β Per) is in Perseus, and is probably its second-brightest star.

Messier and NGC catalogues

Nebulae, clusters and galaxies are listed by catalogue number. Charles Messier, a French comet hunter, listed about 110 fuzzy objects so he would not mistake them for comets: M31 is number 31 on his list. The much bigger New General Catalogue (NGC), published in 1888, lists 7,840. Many objects have both numbers:

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: Alpha is usually, not always, the brightest star in a constellation: in Orion, Beta (Rigel) is brighter than Alpha (Betelgeuse). Reading a Bayer name ↑
