Wavelength shift

Hydrogen in a laboratory absorbs at fixed wavelengths (see Reading a spectrum). In the light of a galaxy outside the Local Group, the same pattern appears, but every line sits at a longer wavelength, towards the red end: a . The whole pattern moves together, which is how we know they are the same lines.

Galaxies inside the Local Group are left out: gravity holds the group together, so their own motions win, and the Andromeda Galaxy is moving towards us, showing a blueshift. First draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Why: the galaxy is moving away

As a galaxy moves away, it sends out each wave crest from a little further off than the one before, so the crests reach us further apart: the wavelength we receive is longer. Redshift is caused by the galaxy from us, and the faster it recedes, the bigger the shift. Something moving towards us gives a blueshift instead: the same idea as for stars on Reading a spectrum and for gas in Our Galaxy's 21 cm map.

Working out the speed

(wavelength shift) ÷ (emitted wavelength) = v ÷ c

  • wavelength shift = observed wavelength − emitted wavelength
  • emitted wavelength: the laboratory value
  • v: the galaxy's recession velocity, its speed away from us
  • c: the speed of light, (data sheet)

To find v, start from the relation and multiply both sides by c:

v = c × (wavelength shift) ÷ (emitted wavelength)

To find the observed wavelength, multiply both sides by the emitted wavelength to get the shift, then add the shift on:

wavelength shift = (emitted wavelength) × v ÷ c
observed wavelength = emitted wavelength + wavelength shift
