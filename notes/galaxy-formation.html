How galaxies form and change

Two main theories for how galaxies formed, and how collisions change them. Step through each.

Top-down: one giant cloud collapses

In the top-down theory, a whole galaxy forms from one huge cloud of gas that collapses under its own gravity. The oldest stars formed first, while the cloud was still round, which is why globular clusters fill the halo of galaxies like ours.

Bottom-up: small pieces merge

In the bottom-up theory, small clumps form first and gravity merges them, over billions of years, into bigger and bigger galaxies. Very distant galaxies, seen as they were long ago, are smaller and more irregular, which supports it; most astronomers now think galaxies grew this way, perhaps with some top-down collapse too.

Collisions change galaxy types

Collisions change a galaxy's type: two spirals can merge into one elliptical. The stars almost never hit each other, but the gas clouds do. Our Galaxy and the Andromeda Galaxy, the biggest members of the Local Group, are moving towards each other.

How sure are we?

Both theories, the evidence from distant galaxies, that most astronomers now favour bottom-up, that mergers can make ellipticals, that colliding galaxies' stars almost never hit each other, and that the Milky Way and Andromeda may merge in the next few billion years (a recent study put the chance at about one in two within 10 billion years): first draft, to be checked by the teacher.
