Archaeoastronomy: Precession and Monuments

Earth's axis doesn't point the same way forever: it slowly traces a circle among the stars, over about 26,000 years. That single fact changes which star sits at the pole, and it's written into real ancient monuments, from Stonehenge to the Great Pyramid of Giza. The wobble's own mechanism is covered in full on its companion page, Measuring the Sky.

The main axis direction (about 50° east of north) is a first draft, to be checked by the teacher. The sunrise azimuth uses an ideal flat horizon, ignoring the real horizon's height and atmospheric refraction, which shift the true rising point by about a degree.

Precession doesn't move this alignment — it changes which star sits at the pole, not how far round the horizon the solstice Sun rises from a given latitude. See the exam tip below.

Alignments: when does the Sun line up?

A straight passage lines up with the Sun when its direction matches the passage's own bearing, to within the passage's width. The Sun's rising point drifts all year, so that match happens more than once — try the tunnel below, built in the style of exam question June 2025 Paper 1 Q6.

Switch to Sunset: the Sun sets at 360° minus its sunrise azimuth, and can shine in through the tunnel's other end. Near the equinoxes the azimuth shifts only about 0.6° a day, which is why a match lasts several days, not one. This uses the standard sunrise correction for the Sun's apparent size and atmospheric refraction, unlike the simplified flat-horizon case used for Stonehenge above.

The tunnel's bearing line crosses this curve twice a year, near spring and near autumn — and the matching sunset line crosses it twice more, through the tunnel's other end. So a straight tunnel like this one lines up with the Sun at least twice a year, on several days each time, not once.

How to answer a question like this

Five skills examiners expect, each worth a quick look before the exam needs them. This summary is general exam technique, not specification content — first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

(a) Converting degrees and minutes to decimal degrees

A degree splits into 60 minutes of arc, so divide the minutes by 60 and add them to the whole degrees: 30′ is 0.5°, 45′ is 0.75°, and 1° is 60′.

(b) Plotting a graph examiners will give credit for

  • Choose a scale that uses more than half the grid in both directions — not a tiny cluster of points in one corner.
  • Label both axes with their units in brackets, for example "Date" and "Azimuth (degrees)".
  • Plot every point accurately, then draw a single smooth curve or line of best fit through them — not dot-to-dot.

(c) Interpolating between two plotted points

To estimate a value between two plotted points, read halfway between them (or use the fraction of the way across, if it's not exactly halfway). If the azimuth is 102.2° on 27 Feb and 100.4° on 2 Mar, the value halfway between the dates is about 101.3° — the mean of the two.

(d) Taking a bearing from a diagram

With a protractor, measure the angle clockwise from north. From a grid, measure how far across (the "run") and how far up (the "rise") the line goes, find tan-1(run ÷ rise), and: if the line points into the north-east quarter, that angle is the bearing; into the south-east quarter, the bearing is 90° plus the angle measured from east.

(e) "Evaluate" questions worth 6 marks

The top band needs all three: relevant knowledge used throughout, not just the given data (for example, the Sun's declination rises through April, so it rises further north of east and the sunrise azimuth falls — which is why a table's azimuths decrease); a clear, logical line of reasoning connecting each point to the next; and a conclusion that the reasoning actually supports, not just a repeated assertion.

Worked example: a passage at 100°

A tour guide claims a straight passage at bearing 100° only lets the sunrise shine through once a year, on 2 March. The table below is generated the same way as the diagram above, for a different bearing and a different fortnight — plot it, then evaluate the claim.

The passage is straight and points roughly ENE/WSW (bearing 100°, close to due east). The Sun's azimuth on 2 March (about 100.4°) is very close to the passage's bearing, so the guide's claim is plausible for that date. But the azimuth changes slowly — about 0.6° a day near the equinox — and the passage has width, so it would shine through on several days close to 2 March, not on that single day. The Sun rises at every angle between about north-east and south-east over the year, so the same azimuth occurs again in autumn, around 10–11 October. And at sunset the Sun can shine in from the passage's other end, when its setting azimuth lines up with bearing 280° (100° + 180°) — which happens around 3–4 April and 8 September. Conclusion: it's plausible that the passage lines up with the Sun close to 2 March, but wrong that this happens on only one occasion a year — on this evidence it happens at least four times, several days each time.

    Exam tips

    Common exam confusion: Stonehenge's solstice alignment is not a precession example. Precession changes the direction Earth's axis points, not how much it's tilted, so it doesn't change how far north or south the solstice Sun rises or sets from a given latitude. That's set by the axial tilt itself, which does also change, but far more slowly (over about 41,000 years) and separately from precession. Stonehenge: the solstice-sunrise alignment ↑

    Common exam confusion: it's tempting to assume Polaris has always been the pole star, since it's the pole star now. The ancient Egyptians who built the pyramids used Thuban instead — Polaris was tens of degrees from the pole in their time, no use at all for finding true north. Axial precession ↑

    Common exam confusion: precession does not change the shapes of constellations. The stars themselves barely move relative to each other over a human lifetime (that tiny drift is proper motion, a separate and far slower effect). What precession changes is the direction Earth's axis points against that essentially fixed backdrop of stars. Axial precession ↑
