Archaeoastronomy: Precession and Monuments

Earth's axis doesn't point the same way forever: it slowly traces a circle among the stars, over about 26,000 years. That single fact changes which star sits at the pole, and it's written into real ancient monuments, from Stonehenge to the Great Pyramid of Giza. The wobble's own mechanism, and an interactive year-by-year explorer, are covered on its companion page, Measuring the Sky.

Axial precession, briefly

Earth spins like a slightly lopsided top. The Sun's and Moon's gravity pull on its equatorial bulge, and instead of straightening the tilt, the spinning Earth responds by slowly swinging the direction its axis points around in a circle — a motion called . The tilt itself stays close to 23.4° throughout; only the direction changes, taking about 26,000 years to go once round.

Because the axis points somewhere new, the whole sky's apparent long-term geometry drifts with it, even though nothing about a single day or year looks any different:

  • The pole star changes. Whichever bright star happens to lie near the axis's direction is the pole star of that era — and over centuries, a different one takes over.
  • The equinox points drift westward along the ecliptic, by about 1° every 72 years, slowly shifting which constellation the Sun sits in at the moment of the spring equinox.
  • Every star's right ascension and declination shift slowly too, which is why star catalogues state the date (epoch) their coordinates are measured for, such as J2000.

This page is about what that means for a handful of real monuments. The wobble's cause, a full interactive diagram, and the list of pole stars through history are covered in depth on Measuring the Sky.

The pole star then and now: Thuban to Polaris

Around 2800 BCE, the faint star Thuban (Alpha Draconis) sat within about 0.2° of the north celestial pole — as close as Polaris sits today. By the time the Great Pyramid was built, around 2560 BCE, precession had already carried the pole about away from Thuban — not as tight a fit as a couple of centuries earlier, but still far closer than any alternative: Polaris, at the same date, was about from the pole, nowhere near useful for finding north.

Today the position is reversed. Polaris sits about from the pole — closing in further until around AD 2100 — while Thuban has drifted roughly away. Ancient Egyptian astronomers aligning a building to true north would have sighted on Thuban; using Polaris the same way would have pointed them noticeably off true north.

Stonehenge: the solstice-sunrise alignment

Stonehenge was built and reworked in stages from around 3000 BCE, with its famous ring of great sarsen stones and trilithons raised around 2500 BCE. Its main axis, running out along the Avenue past the outlying Heel Stone, is aligned with sunrise on the midsummer solstice as seen from the centre of the monument. Face the opposite way, back through the great trilithon, and the same axis frames sunset on the midwinter solstice instead.

That single axis marking both events is deliberate: the solstices are the two days the rising and setting Sun reach their most extreme points along the horizon, so a monument built to track them needed no calendar at all — just a clear horizon and a fixed line of sight.

The Great Pyramid's star shafts

The Great Pyramid at Giza, built around 2560 BCE at a latitude very close to 30°N, contains four narrow shafts running out from the King's and Queen's Chambers — too narrow to be ventilation, and long thought to have had a symbolic, sky-facing purpose. Each shaft holds a fixed angle, cut once and never adjustable, so each one points at a single, unchanging direction in the sky.

For the King's Chamber's two shafts, that fixed direction has a long-proposed match in the sky of 2560 BCE. The northern shaft, tilted about 32–33° above the horizon, points into the circumpolar region close to where Thuban sat near the pole at the time. The southern shaft, tilted about 45°, points to where Orion's Belt — specifically its brightest star, Alnitak — would have crossed the meridian at its lowest point above the horizon in that era.

Because of precession, neither target still sits where it did. Thuban is no longer near the pole, and Orion's Belt now culminates at a different altitude from Giza than it did 4,500 years ago. The shafts still point exactly where they always have — it's the sky around their fixed targets that has moved on.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: Stonehenge's solstice alignment is not a precession example. Precession changes the direction Earth's axis points, not how much it's tilted, so it doesn't change how far north or south the solstice Sun rises or sets from a given latitude. That's set by the axial tilt itself, which does also change, but far more slowly (over about 41,000 years) and separately from precession. Stonehenge: the solstice-sunrise alignment ↑

Common exam confusion: it's tempting to assume Polaris has always been the pole star, since it's the pole star now. The ancient Egyptians who built the pyramids used Thuban instead — Polaris was tens of degrees from the pole in their time, no use at all for finding true north. The pole star then and now ↑

Common exam confusion: precession does not change the shapes of constellations. The stars themselves barely move relative to each other over a human lifetime (that tiny drift is proper motion, a separate and far slower effect).
