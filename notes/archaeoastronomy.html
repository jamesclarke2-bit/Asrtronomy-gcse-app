Archaeoastronomy: Precession and Monuments

Earth's axis doesn't point the same way forever: it slowly traces a circle among the stars, over about 26,000 years. That single fact changes which star sits at the pole, and it's written into real ancient monuments, from Stonehenge to the Great Pyramid of Giza. The wobble's own mechanism is covered in full on its companion page, Measuring the Sky.

Stonehenge: the sunrise alignment

Stonehenge's main axis points towards where the Sun rises at the midsummer solstice. Move the slider through the year to see the sunrise direction swing between the two solstices, crossing due east at the equinoxes.

The main axis direction (about 50° east of north) is a first draft, to be checked by the teacher. The sunrise azimuth uses an ideal flat horizon, ignoring the real horizon's height and atmospheric refraction, which shift the true rising point by about a degree.

Precession doesn't move this alignment — it changes which star sits at the pole, not how far round the horizon the solstice Sun rises from a given latitude. See the exam tip below.

    Exam tips

    Common exam confusion: Stonehenge's solstice alignment is not a precession example. Precession changes the direction Earth's axis points, not how much it's tilted, so it doesn't change how far north or south the solstice Sun rises or sets from a given latitude. That's set by the axial tilt itself, which does also change, but far more slowly (over about 41,000 years) and separately from precession. Stonehenge: the solstice-sunrise alignment ↑

    Common exam confusion: it's tempting to assume Polaris has always been the pole star, since it's the pole star now. The ancient Egyptians who built the pyramids used Thuban instead — Polaris was tens of degrees from the pole in their time, no use at all for finding true north. Axial precession ↑

    Common exam confusion: precession does not change the shapes of constellations. The stars themselves barely move relative to each other over a human lifetime (that tiny drift is proper motion, a separate and far slower effect). What precession changes is the direction Earth's axis points against that essentially fixed backdrop of stars. Axial precession ↑
