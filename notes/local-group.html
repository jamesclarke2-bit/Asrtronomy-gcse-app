The Local Group and beyond

Our Galaxy has neighbours, held close by gravity, and beyond them lie far bigger crowds.

Our Local Group

The Local Group is the group of galaxies gravitationally linked to the Milky Way: their gravity holds them together. Here it is from above:

Andromeda is the biggest, a spiral a little larger than ours. The LMC's distance comes from a Cepheid, as worked out on Variable stars. M31's catalogue number is explained on Catalogues and star names, and the Milky Way's shape on Our Galaxy.

More than 50 galaxies (about 80 known), most of them dwarfs, about 3 million pc (10 million light-years) across, the SMC about 62,000 pc and M33 about 850,000 pc away, and the galaxies' types and places on the map: first draft, to be checked by the teacher. The LMC (50,000 pc) and M31 (760,000 pc) distances come from the Cepheids on Variable stars; light-years use the data sheet's 1 pc = 3.26 light-years.

Groups, clusters and superclusters

Zoom out, one step at a time:

Why the crowds? Gravity pulls galaxies towards each other, so over billions of years they have gathered into groups, groups and galaxies into clusters, and clusters into superclusters. On the largest scale, superclusters lie along filaments round huge, nearly empty voids: a cosmic web.
