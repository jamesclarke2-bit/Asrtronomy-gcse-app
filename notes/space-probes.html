Space probes

Telescopes look from afar; a space probe goes there.

Four ways to visit

A is a robot spacecraft, with no crew, sent to study another body close up. Choose a kind of probe to see how it meets its target.

Fly-by

  • ✓ Cheapest kind of visit: it needs no fuel to slow down.
  • ✓ Can reach the most distant targets, and pass several on one trip.
  • ✗ Only a brief close look: a few hours near the target.
  • ✗ Cannot follow changes over time.

Orbiter

  • ✓ Studies the target for months or years, mapping the whole surface.
  • ✓ Can follow changes, such as weather and seasons.
  • ✗ Must carry extra fuel to slow down into orbit, so costs more.
  • ✗ Cannot touch or sample the surface.

Impactor

  • ✓ Reveals what lies beneath the surface.
  • ✓ The crash can be watched by a nearby craft and from Earth.
  • ✗ Destroyed in the crash: a one-off measurement.
  • ✗ Needs a very precise aim.

Lander

  • ✓ Measures the surface directly, close up.
  • ✓ Can sample material and do experiments on the spot.
  • ✗ Hardest and most expensive: landing can fail.
  • ✗ Studies only one small spot.

One mission of each kind

Fly-by: New Horizons

Target: the dwarf planet Pluto, then Arrokoth, a small icy body further out in the . Launched 2006; flew past Pluto on 14 July 2015, and Arrokoth on 1 January 2019.

  • A huge, smooth, heart-shaped plain of frozen nitrogen on Pluto, with very few craters, so its surface is young.
  • Mountains of water ice, and a thin, hazy atmosphere.
  • Arrokoth is two rounded lumps joined gently together.

Mission dates and discoveries: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Orbiter: Juno

Target: Jupiter, on an orbit passing over both poles. Launched 2011; in orbit since 2016.

  • Clusters of giant storms (cyclones) around both of Jupiter’s poles.
  • A core that is not a small, sharp ball but spread out and mixed into the layers above it.
  • A very strong, lopsided magnetic field.

Or Dawn, which orbited the asteroid Vesta from 2011, then the dwarf planet Ceres from 2015 (launched 2007): the first probe to orbit two different bodies beyond the Earth–Moon system. It found that bright spots on Ceres are salt left behind by salty water from below the surface.

Mission dates and discoveries: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Impactor: Deep Impact

Target: Comet Tempel 1. Launched 2005; its impactor hit the comet on 4 July 2005, watched by the fly-by craft that carried it.

  • The crash threw out a huge cloud of very fine, powdery dust: the comet’s core is loose and porous.
  • More dust than ice: more an “icy dirtball” than a “dirty snowball”.
  • Water ice and carbon-based (organic) material.

Mission dates and discoveries: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Lander: Philae

Target: Comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, carried there by the Rosetta orbiter. Rosetta launched 2004; Philae landed on 12 November 2014.

  • Carbon-based (organic) molecules on the comet, some never found on a comet before.
  • Its harpoons failed to fire, so it bounced: the surface was harder than expected beneath its dust.
  • It came to rest in shadow, so it ran out of power after about 60 hours.

A probe or a telescope?

For a probe

  • ✓ Far more detail, from close up.
  • ✓ Can measure things a telescope cannot, such as a magnetic field, or touch the surface.
  • ✓ Sees sides that never face the Earth.

For a telescope

  • ✓ Studies many targets, for years, and is far cheaper per target.
  • ✓ Results start straight away; a probe can take years to arrive.
  • ✓ Can be repaired and upgraded; a probe cannot.

Getting away from the Earth

To leave the Earth for good, a probe must reach the Earth’s , about 11.2 km/s. Slower, and gravity pulls it back. Try a launch speed:

Only a rocket can reach that speed. The push must last for minutes, through thin air and then the vacuum of space.

  • A jet engine takes in air: it needs oxygen from the air to burn its fuel. Space has no air.
  • A rocket carries its own oxidiser (the oxygen supply) as well as its fuel.
  • It throws hot exhaust gas out backwards very fast, which pushes the rocket forwards. It needs nothing to push against, so it works in space.
