Black holes: the evidence

Nothing escapes a black hole, not even light. So how do we know any are there?

Seen by what it does

A black hole can't be seen, but its gravity changes things nearby. Here a star is losing gas to an unseen partner:

So astronomers look for a black hole's effects: gas that gets extremely hot as it falls in, and stars that orbit something heavy that gives out no light. How black holes form is on Massive stars and their ends.

Evidence 1: X-ray binaries

In an X-ray binary, gas pulled off a star spirals into an , heated to millions of degrees, so it gives out X-rays, picked up by space telescopes. The visible star's orbit gives the unseen partner's mass:

Cygnus X-1 (the unseen black hole about 20 times the Sun's mass; the visible supergiant heavier, about 40; the pair go round each other every 5.6 days; identified as a black hole in the early 1970s), gas heated to millions of degrees, and the neutron-star limit of about 3 times the Sun's mass: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Evidence 2: stars at the centre of the Milky Way

Dust hides the centre of our Galaxy in visible light, but infrared passes through. Astronomers have tracked stars there for decades. Each orbits something they cannot see. Follow the star S2:

The orbit gives the hidden mass. In Solar System units, Kepler's third law says M = a³ ÷ T² (M in Sun masses, a, half the orbit's longest width, in AU, and T in years). For S2, M = 1,000³ ÷ 16² ≈ 3.9 million Suns, packed inside S2's closest approach yet giving out no starlight. Only a supermassive black hole fits.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the X-rays come from gas heated as it falls towards the black hole, from outside it, not from the black hole itself. Evidence 1: X-ray binaries ↑

Common exam confusion: an unseen partner is a black hole only if it is above about 3 times the Sun's mass, too heavy for a neutron star. Evidence 1: X-ray binaries ↑
