Magnitude: how bright is a star?

How bright a star looks (apparent magnitude) is not the same as how bright it actually is (absolute magnitude) — distance changes one but not the other. Three tiers, from the idea itself up to the distance modulus formula used at Astro Challenge level. Builds on the parsec from Parallax and the Parsec.

Absolute magnitude never changes as the star moves — only apparent magnitude does. At exactly 10 pc the two are equal: m = M, which is exactly why 10 pc is chosen as the standard distance.

Tier 1 · The idea

Why distance matters: the inverse-square law

The same lamp light, spread over a bigger area the farther it travels — so any one small patch of it catches less.

One unit square's share of the lamp's light, at each distance
DistanceBrightness (fraction of that at d)Magnitude change from d

Double the distance and the same light covers 4× the area, so any one patch gets a quarter of the brightness — a magnitude change of 2.5 log10(4), about 1.5 magnitudes fainter. This is the same inverse-square law behind the distance modulus formula below.

Sirius (apparent magnitude −1.46) looks noticeably brighter in our sky than Rigel (apparent magnitude +0.13) — about times brighter. But Rigel is actually around times more luminous than Sirius; it only looks fainter from Earth because it sits roughly times farther away. Looking bright and being bright really are different things. m1 is the fainter star here (the larger magnitude) and m2 the brighter one, so a positive difference always gives a ratio above 1 — the brighter star's own flux, I2, divided by the fainter one's, I1.

Tier 3 · Astro Challenge

Distance modulus: linking brightness to distance

Brightness falls with the inverse-square law, so a star 10 times farther away looks exactly 100 times fainter — and 100 times fainter is, by definition, exactly 5 magnitudes. That single link gives the formula this tier runs on:

m − M = 5 log10(d / 10 pc)

This is one of only six equations the exam's own data sheet gives you directly — so although it's the hardest tier on this page, it is not beyond the specification.

Distance modulus calculator

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: a smaller or more negative magnitude means a brighter object — the scale runs backwards from how numbers normally work. Magnitude -1 is brighter than magnitude +2. The magnitude scale ↑

Common exam confusion: apparent magnitude (m) depends on distance; absolute magnitude (M) does not — M is defined as the apparent magnitude a star would have from exactly 10 pc away, whatever its real distance actually is. Apparent versus absolute magnitude ↑

Common exam link: a smaller parallax angle means a larger distance (see Parallax and the Parsec), and a larger distance means a fainter apparent magnitude — the same "smaller number, farther away" direction runs through both pages, just with different formulas. Distance modulus ↑
