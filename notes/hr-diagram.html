The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram

Plot every star by how hot it is and how much light it gives out, and the stars fall into a few clear groups.

The diagram

Each star is one point: surface temperature across, and or up.

Exam questions may use either y-axis: luminosity (Sun = 1) or absolute magnitude. Both put the brightest stars at the top, so on the magnitude scale the most negative values are at the top. Temperature increasing to the left is the standard way round.

    Checklist for a sketched answer

    • Both axes labelled with quantities: surface temperature across; luminosity or absolute magnitude up.
    • Temperature decreasing to the right.
    • Main sequence: a diagonal band from top left to bottom right.
    • Giants top right; supergiants across the top.
    • White dwarfs bottom left.
    • The Sun in the middle of the main sequence.

    Distances from the HR diagram

    If a star is on the main sequence, its spectral type tells you where it sits on the band, so you can read its absolute magnitude M off the diagram. Comparing M with its measured m gives its distance d, in , from the data sheet formula M = m + 5 − 5 log d (see Magnitude: how bright is a star?).

    Worked example: a G2 main-sequence star has m = +9.8. The band gives M ≈ +4.8, so m − M = 5. Then 5 log d = 5 + 5 = 10, log d = 2 and d = 100 pc. The band runs from M = +3.8 to +5.8, giving 63 pc to 158 pc: a range of 95 pc, so the uncertainty is half of it, about ±48 pc.

      This only works if we know the star is on the main sequence: a giant of the same type is far more luminous, so its distance would come out much too small.
