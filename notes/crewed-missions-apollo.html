Crewed missions and Apollo

What people can do in space that robots cannot, what it costs to keep them alive, and how the Apollo programme took twelve people to walk on the Moon and brought them home.

Send people, or send a probe?

A crewed mission carries people; a probe is a robot (see Space probes). People can think on the spot, choose the best samples and fix things that break: crews repaired and upgraded the Hubble Space Telescope, which later took the Deep Field (see Evidence for the Big Bang). But a crew needs air, water, food and warmth, has to come home, and is at risk from accidents and radiation, so crewed missions cost far more and have only gone as far as the Moon. A probe can travel for years to the outer planets.

Apollo: the race and the rocket

A drawing, not to scale across its width.

In 1961 the Soviet Union put the first person in space, Yuri Gagarin. Weeks later, US President John F. Kennedy set the goal of landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely before the end of the decade: the Apollo programme. It cost about $25 billion at the time. Its rocket, the Saturn V, burned its stages one after another, each dropping away when empty so the rest had less mass to push; only the small spacecraft on top went to the Moon.

Yuri Gagarin, 1961; Kennedy's goal, set weeks later in 1961; the cost of about $25 billion at the time; the Saturn V being about 110 m tall with three stages: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Three spacecraft in one

The Command and Service Modules flew joined together as one craft until just before the end. Three astronauts travelled in the Command Module; two of them went down to the surface in the Lunar Module while the third stayed in orbit.

    What Apollo left behind and brought back

    Not to scale.

    The crews left reflectors on the Moon. Timing a laser pulse there and back measures the Earth-Moon distance to within centimetres or better. They also brought back hundreds of kilograms of rock and soil. The rocks are extremely dry, with only traces of water found later, and they are part of the evidence for how the Moon formed (see where the Moon came from).
