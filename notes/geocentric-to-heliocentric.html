From Earth-Centred to Sun-Centred

For over a thousand years, astronomy put Earth at the centre of everything. This page follows how that changed: the geocentric model and the epicycles bolted onto it to make it fit the sky, Tycho Brahe's unprecedented naked-eye precision, Kepler finding ellipses in Brahe's own data, and the two telescope observations Galileo made that the old model simply couldn't explain.

Early geocentric models

The earliest systematic models of the universe placed , with the Moon, Sun, planets and stars all moving around it. This matched how the sky actually looks from the ground — everything does appear to circle overhead — and it stayed the accepted picture for well over a thousand years.

The most complete version was Ptolemy's, from around AD 150. Each planet travelled on a — its main circular path around Earth — but Ptolemy didn't centre that circle exactly on Earth, and didn't even make the planet move at a constant rate around it. Instead he measured the planet's motion as uniform around a separate, nearby point called the . It was a deliberately engineered fit to the observed sky, not a physical explanation of why the planets moved that way.

Ptolemy's epicycles

A planet on a single, simple circle around Earth would just drift one way across the sky at a steady brightness. Real planets don't: they periodically loop backwards for a few weeks or months — — and visibly brighten and dim as they do. To reproduce both effects, Ptolemy gave each planet a second, smaller circle: the . The planet rides on the epicycle, and the epicycle's own centre rides on the deferent.

This models an outer ("superior") planet — Mars — using its real orbital period. Historically, Ptolemy gave each outer planet's epicycle a period of exactly one year, matching the Sun's own apparent yearly motion, while the deferent took the planet's own real period: here, Mars's 1.88 years. Only the epicycle's size is adjustable below; real Ptolemaic models tuned a size for each planet individually to match its observed loops, but the one-year epicycle period for outer planets was historically consistent.

This is also a simplified epicycle: Ptolemy's real model additionally offset the deferent's centre away from Earth and measured each planet's motion as uniform around a separate equant point, not around Earth itself. Only the epicycle is shown here, to demonstrate that one principle on its own.

Every outer planet needs an epicycle with exactly this one-year period, matching the Sun's own yearly cycle around the sky. In Ptolemy's Earth-centred model, that's an unexplained coincidence, repeated separately for every single outer planet. In Copernicus's Sun-centred model it isn't a coincidence at all: that shared "epicycle" is just Earth's own orbit, which is why the retrograde loops appear in the first place. See Copernicus and Kepler below.

The real cause of retrograde motion is completely different, and far simpler: Earth and the other planets orbit the Sun at different speeds, so Earth periodically "overtakes" an outer planet, which briefly appears to move backwards against the stars as it does. See Retrograde Motion and Planetary Alignments for that Sun-centred explanation of the very same effect this epicycle is reproducing geometrically, without explaining it.

Tycho Brahe's observations

Tycho Brahe didn't propose a new model himself — his own compromise cosmology still kept Earth at the centre. His real contribution was observational: for decades, working entirely before the telescope existed, he measured the positions of the planets, Mars especially, with a precision no one had achieved before. His naked-eye measurements were accurate to about one arcminute, a small fraction of the Moon's width in the sky.

That precision is what mattered. A model that's only roughly right can look fine against rough data. Brahe's data was good enough to show up small, real discrepancies between where Mars actually was and where a circular orbit predicted it should be — exactly the discrepancies Kepler would later need.

Copernicus and Kepler

In 1543, Copernicus proposed a Sun-centred mathematical model: the planets, including Earth, orbiting the Sun. It was a genuine break from a geocentric universe, but Copernicus still used circular orbits, so his model needed epicycles of its own to fit the observed sky closely — it wasn't yet simpler than Ptolemy's, just differently centred.

Kepler, working after Brahe's death with Brahe's own precise Mars data, spent years trying to make that data fit a circular orbit and consistently failed by a small but real margin. Allowing the orbit to be an ellipse instead removed the discrepancy completely. That became the first of Kepler's three laws of planetary motion, and finally gave the heliocentric model a mathematical shape that fit the sky better than Ptolemy's ever had. See Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion for all three laws in full, with an interactive diagram.

Galileo's telescopic evidence

Everything so far was mathematical: better and better ways of fitting a model to positions measured by eye. Galileo, observing with one of the first astronomical telescopes in 1609–1610, added something genuinely new — direct visual evidence the old model couldn't explain at all.

  • Jupiter's four largest moons. Galileo watched four points of light change position around Jupiter night after night, clearly orbiting Jupiter itself, not Earth. That didn't prove the Sun-centred model, but it broke the geocentric model's core assumption that everything in the universe orbits Earth.
  • The phases of Venus. Galileo observed Venus passing through a full set of phases, from a thin crescent through to nearly full, exactly like the Moon. In a strict Earth-centred model, Venus stays roughly between Earth and the Sun at all times, so the Sun can only ever light the side facing away from Earth — at most a crescent. A full range of phases is only possible if Venus orbits the Sun, and sometimes sits on the far side of it from Earth, fully lit.

Unlike Brahe's and Kepler's work, this wasn't about fitting numbers more closely — it was direct observation ruling an entire class of models out.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: epicycles, deferents and equants are three different parts of the same geocentric model, not interchangeable terms. The deferent is the main circle; the equant is an off-centre point used to make motion around the deferent appear uniform; the epicycle is the separate small circle added specifically to produce retrograde loops and brightness changes.
