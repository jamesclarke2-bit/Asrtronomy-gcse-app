From Earth-Centred to Sun-Centred

For over a thousand years, astronomy put Earth at the centre of everything. This page follows how that changed, diagram by diagram: epicycles, two rival models of the same sky, a timeline, Venus's phases, Galileo's telescope, and Kepler's ellipse.

Two models

The same real positions, drawn two ways: Ptolemy and Copernicus agreed on what the sky showed, but disagreed about what sat still at the centre.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: epicycles, deferents and equants are three different parts of the same geocentric model, not interchangeable terms. The deferent is the main circle; the equant is an off-centre point used to make motion around the deferent appear uniform; the epicycle is the separate small circle added specifically to produce retrograde loops and brightness changes. Ptolemy's epicycles ↑

Common exam confusion: Brahe didn't propose the heliocentric model, and didn't use a telescope — it didn't exist yet. His contribution was the precision of his naked-eye observational data, which Kepler used after Brahe's death. Brahe to Kepler ↑

Common exam confusion: Jupiter's moons showed that not everything orbits Earth, a general point against geocentrism. It didn't by itself prove the Sun-centred model. Galileo and Jupiter ↑

Common exam confusion: Venus's phases are the more specific, direct argument against geocentrism: a full range of phases is only geometrically possible if Venus orbits the Sun, not Earth. Venus: the test ↑
