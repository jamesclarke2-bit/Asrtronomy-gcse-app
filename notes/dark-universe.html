Dark matter, dark energy and the future

Most of the Universe is something we cannot see. How we know it is there, why it is so hard to find, and what it means for how the Universe might end.

Dark matter: galaxies spin too fast

Stars and gas in the Milky Way orbit its centre at about 220 km/s (see Our Galaxy). If only the matter we can see were pulling, orbits far from the centre would be slower, as the outer planets are slower than the inner ones. Instead the speed stays flat, so there must be far more mass than we can see, spread through a huge halo round the galaxy: dark matter. It matters because its gravity holds galaxies and clusters together and helped them form (see How galaxies form and change).

The two curves are schematic shapes. Other evidence: galaxies in clusters move too fast to be held together by their visible matter, and gravitational lensing (light bent by mass) shows more mass than we can see. It might be unknown particles, the favoured idea, or dark objects such as black holes and dead stars. First draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Dark energy: the expansion is speeding up

Very distant exploding stars (supernovae of a kind with a known brightness) look fainter than expected, so they are further away than a steady expansion would have carried them: the expansion of the Universe is speeding up (see Hubble's law). Whatever drives this is called dark energy. Its nature is unknown; it may be energy belonging to empty space itself. Together, dark matter and dark energy make up about 95% of the Universe.

The speeding up was found from distant supernovae in the late 1990s; the shares of about 5%, 27% and 68%, and the idea of the energy of empty space: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Why they are so hard to find

Almost everything in astronomy is found by the light it gives out or absorbs (see Reading a spectrum). Dark matter does neither, so telescopes cannot see it at all: it shows itself only through its gravity, on stars, galaxies and light. Dark energy is found only through the expansion speeding up. Experiments built to catch dark matter particles directly have so far found nothing.

That direct searches (for example, detectors deep underground) have found nothing so far: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Possible futures

Gravity from matter slows the expansion; dark energy speeds it up. Current models predict different futures depending on the balance between them: with enough matter, the expansion stops and the Universe collapses; with less, it expands for ever, more and more slowly; with dark energy winning, it speeds up for ever. These are models, not certainties.

Current measurements favour speeding up for ever, dark energy winning; the names "Big Crunch" and the curves' timings are from simple models: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.
