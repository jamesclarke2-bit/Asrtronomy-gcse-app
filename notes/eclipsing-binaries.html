Eclipsing binaries

When two stars orbit each other edge-on to us, each passes in front of the other, and the light we receive dips.

Eclipses make the dips

Star A is the hotter star; star B is larger and cooler. Follow positions 1 to 4 down to the .

  1. Both stars fully visible: the brightest.
  2. The hotter star hidden: the deep dip, the primary eclipse.
  3. Both stars visible again.
  4. The cooler star hidden: the shallow dip, the secondary eclipse.

Why the dips differ

When the same area of star is covered, more light is lost if the hidden star is the hotter one, because each square metre of a hotter star gives out more light. So the deeper dip is when the hotter star is behind.

A flat-bottomed dip means the smaller star is completely hidden behind the larger one, or completely in front of it, for a while. A rounded or V-shaped dip means a partial eclipse. Eclipses only happen if we see the orbit nearly edge-on; tilt it and the dips shrink, then vanish. Most binaries are not lined up like this, so most show no eclipses at all.

Finding the period

Common mistake: the period is the time from one deep (primary) dip to the next deep dip. From a deep dip to the next shallow dip is only half the period.

Drag the markers to two deep dips, count the whole cycles between them, and divide.
