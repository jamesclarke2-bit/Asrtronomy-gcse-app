Reading a spectrum

Spread a star's light out into its colours and it carries a code: which elements the star contains, how hot it is, and whether it is moving towards or away from us. How astronomers record that code, and how to read it.

Three kinds of spectrum

Switch the parts on and off and watch which spectrum the spectrograph records.

Result
  • Continuous: a hot, dense source gives every wavelength.
  • Emission: a glowing thin gas, seen against a dark background, gives bright lines only.
  • Absorption: cooler gas in front of a hot source removes the same wavelengths, leaving dark lines.

A star's absorption lines come from cooler gas in its outer layers: the hot, dense inside shines with a continuous spectrum, and the thinner, cooler gas above absorbs particular wavelengths on the way out.

Radial velocity: lines that shift

Motion along our line of sight (the star's ) shifts the whole line pattern.

Lines move to longer wavelengths if the star is receding (a ), and to shorter wavelengths if it is approaching (a ). A bigger shift means a faster speed.

Recording the light: digital sensors

Modern telescopes record light with a digital sensor, not photographic film or the eye. Each turns arriving into electrical charge; the charges are read out as numbers and stored as a data file that astronomers process on a computer. On a spectrograph, each column of pixels sees one wavelength, so the stored numbers are the intensity graph. A longer exposure collects more light.
