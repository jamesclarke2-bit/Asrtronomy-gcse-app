Tides

How the Moon's gravity, helped a little by the Sun's, raises two bulges of water on Earth, why that gives most places two high and two low tides a day, and why the tides are biggest at new and full Moon and smallest at the quarters.

Why there are two tidal bulges

The Moon's gravity pulls on every part of Earth, but not equally: gravity gets weaker with distance. The side of Earth facing the Moon is pulled a little more strongly than Earth's centre, and the far side a little less. It's this difference in pull across Earth, the , that raises the tides, not the pull itself.

  • On the side facing the Moon, the water is pulled towards the Moon more than Earth is, so it heaps up into a bulge.
  • On the far side, Earth is pulled towards the Moon more than the water is, so the water is left behind as a second bulge.
  • In between, at right angles to the Moon, water is drawn away towards the bulges, and the sea is low.

As Earth spins, any one place is carried through both bulges and both low regions, so most places get two high tides and two low tides a day. The Moon moves on along its orbit while Earth turns, so it takes about for a place to come back under the Moon: the tides come roughly 12 hours 25 minutes apart, and about 50 minutes later each day. That's also why a calendar day occasionally has only one high tide.

The Sun's smaller share

The Sun raises tides in the same way. Its overall pull on Earth is about times stronger than the Moon's, but it's so far away that its pull is almost the same on both sides of Earth. The difference across Earth is what counts, so the Sun's tidal effect is only about of the Moon's: a bit less than half.

Spring tides and neap tides

Because the Sun raises its own, smaller pair of bulges, the size of the tides depends on where the Sun and Moon are compared with each other, which is exactly what sets the Moon's phase.

Spring tides

At new Moon and full Moon, the Sun, Earth and Moon are in a line. The Sun's bulges line up with the Moon's and add to them, giving higher high tides and lower low tides: the largest .

Neap tides

At first and last quarter, the Sun and Moon are at right angles as seen from Earth. The Sun's bulges fall where the Moon's low water is, partly filling it in, giving lower high tides and higher low tides: the smallest tidal range.

There's a new or full Moon about every 15 days, so spring and neap tides each come round twice a month, alternating about a week apart. In this simple model a spring tide's range is about times a neap tide's.

Real tides follow this pattern, but coastlines and the shape of the sea floor change the details. High tide at a real port doesn't come exactly when the Moon is overhead, a few places get only one tide a day, and the biggest spring tides usually arrive a day or two after the new or full Moon. The extra-large spring tides at a supermoon are covered on Moon Phases too.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: there are two bulges, not one. The far side bulges too, because the Moon pulls Earth's centre more strongly than the water on the far side. Two bulges are why there are two high tides a day. Why there are two tidal bulges ↑

Common exam confusion: spring tides have nothing to do with the season. They happen twice a month, at every new and full Moon, all year round. Spring tides and neap tides ↑

Common exam confusion: neap tides aren't unusually low tides. They're tides with a small range: the high tides are lower than usual, and the low tides are higher. Spring tides and neap tides ↑

Common exam confusion: the Sun pulls on Earth far more strongly than the Moon does, yet the Moon causes most of the tide. Tides come from the difference in pull across Earth, and that falls off much faster with distance than the pull itself. The Sun's smaller share ↑
