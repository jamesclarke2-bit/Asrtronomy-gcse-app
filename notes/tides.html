Spring tides

Neap tides

First and last quarter: Sun and Moon at right angles, bulges partly cancel — the smallest range.

Why there are two tidal bulges

The Moon's gravity pulls harder on the near side of Earth than the far side — that difference, the , is what raises the tides, not the pull itself.

  • Facing the Moon: water is pulled more than Earth itself — a bulge.
  • The far side: Earth is pulled more than the water — a second bulge.
  • In between: water drains towards the bulges — the sea is low.

On a real coastline, the same cycle shows up as the sea's edge moving between a high tide mark and a low tide mark.

Most places get two high tides and two low tides a day, about apart.

The Sun's own pull on Earth is about times stronger than the Moon's, but its tidal effect is only about of the Moon's.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: there are two bulges, not one. The far side bulges too, because the Moon pulls Earth's centre more strongly than the water on the far side. Two bulges are why there are two high tides a day. Why there are two tidal bulges ↑

Common exam confusion: spring tides have nothing to do with the season. They happen twice a month, at every new and full Moon, all year round. Spring tides and neap tides ↑

Common exam confusion: neap tides aren't unusually low tides. They're tides with a small range: the high tides are lower than usual, and the low tides are higher. Spring tides and neap tides ↑

Common exam confusion: the Sun pulls on Earth far more strongly than the Moon does, yet the Moon causes most of the tide. Tides come from the difference in pull across Earth, and that falls off much faster with distance than the pull itself. Why there are two tidal bulges ↑

Common exam link: high tides come about 50 minutes later each day for the same reason the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day: it moves along its orbit while Earth turns. Why there are two tidal bulges ↑
