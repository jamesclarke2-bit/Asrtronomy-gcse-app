Space telescopes

Some of the sky can only be seen from above the atmosphere, so astronomers put telescopes in space.

Why some telescopes must be in space

Each bar shows how far that kind of wave gets down through the atmosphere. The three marked “need space” never reach the ground, apart from a little near ultraviolet.

✓ Reaches the ground   ∼ Partly absorbed (ultraviolet: most absorbed; near ultraviolet reaches the ground)   ✗ Absorbed high in the atmosphere

Gamma rays and X-rays are absorbed high in the atmosphere, far above the highest mountain, and so is most ultraviolet: only the near ultraviolet, just beyond violet light (down to about 300 nm), reaches the ground. So a telescope for them must be above the atmosphere, in space. Much infrared is absorbed too: a high, dry mountain gets some of it (see Infrared telescopes and high-altitude sites), but only space gets all of it. Visible light and radio waves reach the ground.

That X-rays, gamma rays and most ultraviolet are absorbed far above any mountain, that near ultraviolet down to about 300 nm reaches the ground, and that space gets all of the infrared: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Telescopes in orbit

An orbital observing platform is a telescope or detector carried in an orbit: it travels round and round the Earth, or the Sun, above the atmosphere, without needing fuel to stay up. Compare a low orbit with a far one.

    Space or the ground?

    For a space telescope

    • ✓ Every wavelength is available: nothing is absorbed.
    • ✓ No turbulence, so images are sharp.
    • ✓ No weather or clouds.
    • ✓ No day and night: it can observe all the time.

    Against a space telescope

    • ✗ It costs far more to build and launch.
    • ✗ The launch can fail.
    • ✗ Its size is limited by the rocket.
    • ✗ It is hard, or impossible, to repair.
    • ✗ It works for a limited time.

    What gamma-ray, X-ray and ultraviolet astronomy found

    • Gamma-ray bursts: brief, extremely powerful flashes of gamma rays from very distant galaxies, first picked up by detectors on satellites (the Vela satellites, 1967).
    • Black hole accretion discs: gas pulled towards a black hole spirals into a disc and is heated to millions of degrees, so it gives out X-rays (Cygnus X-1 was an early example).
    • Young stars: ultraviolet and X-rays show the structure of a young star's chromosphere (a thin, hot layer above its surface) and corona (its very hot outer atmosphere), far more active than the Sun's (see The Sun: Structure and Features).

    These three discoveries, the Vela satellites (1967) and Cygnus X-1: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.
