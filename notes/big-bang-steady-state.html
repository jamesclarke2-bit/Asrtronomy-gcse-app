Big Bang and Steady State

Two theories both explain why galaxies are moving apart. Step through time and compare them side by side.

Two theories, one expanding Universe

A drawing. Dot: a galaxy. Ring: a new galaxy, formed since the last step. Square and triangle: the same two galaxies, from step 2. The Big Bang began as hot, dense gas: galaxies formed later.

In both theories galaxies are moving apart, as the redshift of distant galaxies shows (see Hubble's law, age and size). In the Big Bang theory, everything began in a hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since, so space thins out; run the expansion backwards and it reaches a beginning (see the age of the Universe). In the Steady State theory, the Universe has no beginning and always looks the same: as it expands, new matter appears in the gaps and forms new galaxies, so the density never changes.

Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle put forward the Steady State theory in 1948, and Hoyle coined the name "Big Bang"; the new matter it needs is far too little to detect directly. First draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Why expansion alone can't decide

A drawing. A four-pointed star marks a quasar.

Both theories predict galaxies moving apart, with more distant galaxies receding faster, so measuring the expansion cannot tell them apart. What differs is the past. Light from far away left long ago, so distant objects show us the Universe as it was. The Big Bang predicts that the early Universe looked different: hotter, denser, with young galaxies. The Steady State predicts it looked just like today. So astronomers look for signs that the Universe has changed: see Evidence for the Big Bang.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the Steady State Universe expands too. What stays the same is its density, because new matter is created as it expands. Two theories, one expanding Universe ↑

Common exam link: redshift and Hubble's law fit both theories; to choose, use evidence that the Universe has changed, such as quasars, the cosmic microwave background and the Hubble Deep Field. Why expansion alone can't decide ↑
