Parallax and the Parsec: Measuring Distances to Stars

How astronomers actually measure the distance to a star: not by eye, but by how much its position appears to shift against far more distant stars as Earth swings from one side of its orbit to the other, six months later. That shift defines the parsec — the unit the next page's brightness-based distances build on.

The shift is magnified many times over here so it can actually be seen — a real parallax angle is a tiny fraction of an arcsecond, far too small to draw to scale.

Hold your thumb out at arm's length, in front of something further away — a wall, a window, a tree. Close your left eye, then your right. Your thumb seems to move back and forth against the distant background, even though it hasn't moved at all: your two eyes are looking at it from two slightly different positions. A nearby star does exactly the same thing against the far more distant background stars, as Earth's orbit carries it around the Sun — just with a much, much longer baseline, and a correspondingly tinier shift.

What exactly is the parallax angle?

Earth, the Sun and the star form a right-angled triangle. The parallax angle, p, is marked where it's actually measured — at the star.

The parallax angle is the tiny angle at the star between the Sun and the Earth.
Not to scale: p is really far smaller than this.

Parallax angle, p

p (arcsec) = 1 ÷ d (pc)

In practice, nobody measures the tiny angle at the star directly. Instead, two photographs of the same patch of sky, taken six months apart, show the nearby star sitting in two slightly different places against the fixed background stars. That total movement between the two photographs is 2p — twice the parallax angle — so p itself is half of what's actually measured on the photographs.

The units: arcseconds and parsecs

Parallax angles are far too small for degrees to be a convenient unit, so astronomers split the degree much further: 1 degree is 60 , and 1 arcminute is 60 — so 1 degree is 3600 arcseconds.

A (pc) is defined directly from this: it's the distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of exactly 1 arcsecond of parallax — AU, about , or about m.

That AU figure matches the exam data sheet's own rounded parsec (3.1 × 10¹³ km) rather than the geometrically exact value, which is about 0.2% smaller — enough to shift the third significant figure (206,667 AU here, against 206,265 AU exactly). The data sheet's own two figures for a parsec, 3.1 × 10¹³ km and 3.26 light years, differ from each other by about 0.5% for the same reason. This page's worked answers use the data sheet's own values throughout; give your own answers to the number of significant figures the question asks for.

That definition rearranges into the formula this whole page runs on:

d (pc) = 1 ÷ p (arcsec)

Limits: why parallax stops working

The further away a star is, the smaller its parallax angle — and at some point it becomes too small to measure at all. From the ground, atmospheric blurring limits telescopes to about arcsec, a distance of about pc.

Beyond each limit, the angle simply can't be measured — parallax runs out, which is exactly why astronomers turn to a star's brightness instead, on the next page.

A measured parallax always comes with an uncertainty, usually given as plus or minus half the range the repeated measurements actually spread across — repeating a measurement makes that spread clearer, it does not make a faulty method accurate. For a small p, that uncertainty translates into a surprisingly wide range of possible distances. Try it:

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the parallax angle p is the angle at the star itself, in the Sun-Earth-star triangle — not the whole movement a photograph shows. Two photographs six months apart show the star moving by 2p, so p is half of that. What exactly is the parallax angle? ↑

Common exam confusion: a parsec is a distance, not a time or an angle — and it's bigger than a light year (1 pc ≈ 3.26 ly), the opposite of what "parsec" sounds like. The units ↑

Common exam link: a smaller parallax angle always means a larger distance (d = 1/p) — the two move in opposite directions, so don't assume a "bigger number" story applies to both. Limits ↑
