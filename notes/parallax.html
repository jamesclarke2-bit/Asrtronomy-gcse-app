Parallax and the Parsec: Measuring Distances to Stars

How astronomers actually measure the distance to a star: not by eye, but by how much its position appears to shift against far more distant stars as Earth swings from one side of its orbit to the other, six months later. That shift defines the parsec — the unit the next page's brightness-based distances build on.

Seeing a star jump: the parallax effect

Hold a finger at arm's length. Close one eye, then the other — the finger seems to jump against the background. Earth does the same trick on a far bigger scale, six months apart. Drag the slider to see how the shift shrinks as the star gets farther away.

The baseline is 1 AU, Sun to Earth — p itself is half the total apparent shift, measured from the Sun to Earth's position, not from one Earth position to the other. Real stellar parallax angles are a tiny fraction of a degree, far too small to draw to scale, so the angle here is exaggerated to stay visible.

Hold your thumb out at arm's length, in front of something further away — a wall, a window, a tree. Close your left eye, then your right. Your thumb seems to jump back and forth against the distant background, even though it hasn't moved at all: your two eyes are looking at it from two slightly different positions. A nearby star does exactly the same thing against the far more distant background stars, as Earth's orbit carries it between two positions six months apart — just with a much, much longer baseline, and a correspondingly tinier shift.

The units: arcseconds and parsecs

Parallax angles are far too small for degrees to be a convenient unit, so astronomers split the degree much further: 1 degree is 60 , and 1 arcminute is 60 — so 1 degree is 3600 arcseconds.

A (pc) is defined directly from this: it's the distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of exactly 1 arcsecond of parallax — AU, about , or about m.

Limits: why parallax stops working

The further away a star is, the smaller its parallax angle — and at some point it becomes too small to measure at all. From the ground, atmospheric blurring limits telescopes to about arcsec, a distance of about pc.

Beyond each limit, the angle simply can't be measured — parallax runs out, which is exactly why astronomers turn to a star's brightness instead, on the next page.

A measured parallax always comes with an uncertainty, usually given as plus or minus half the range the repeated measurements actually spread across — repeating a measurement makes the uncertainty clearer, not the measurement itself more accurate. Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the parallax angle p is half the star's total apparent shift, not the whole thing — p is measured from the Sun to one Earth position, using a 1 AU baseline, even though the two observations themselves are 2 AU apart. Seeing a star jump ↑

Common exam confusion: a parsec is a distance, not a time or an angle — and it's bigger than a light year (1 pc ≈ 3.26 ly), the opposite of what "parsec" sounds like. The units ↑

Common exam link: a smaller parallax angle always means a larger distance (d = 1/p) — the two move in opposite directions, so don't assume a "bigger number" story applies to both. Limits ↑
