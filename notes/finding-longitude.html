Finding longitude at sea

A ship can find its latitude from the stars, but its longitude needs the time at Greenwich. How sailors found it: by the Moon moving against the stars, and by a clock good enough to carry Greenwich time across an ocean.

Longitude is a time difference

The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours: 15° every hour, or 1° every 4 minutes. At sea, a navigator finds local noon, when the Sun is highest. If a clock keeping Greenwich time then reads later than 12:00, the ship is west of Greenwich; earlier, it is east. Each hour of difference is 15° of longitude (how far east or west of Greenwich a place is). Finding local noon from a shadow, with the equation of time, is on Shadows and Sundials. The hard part at sea was knowing the Greenwich time.

The astronomical method: lunar distance

The Moon moves across the stars by about half a degree every hour, about its own width, so the sky works as a clock that every ship can see. With a sextant (an instrument for measuring the angle between two objects), the navigator measures the lunar distance, the angle between the Moon and a bright star, then looks it up in tables to find the Greenwich time at that moment. The tables are needed because the Moon's motion is complicated: its position against the stars at each Greenwich time had to be worked out in advance by astronomers. A small error matters: an angle 1 arcminute out is 2 minutes of time, so half a degree of longitude.

The Moon moving about half a degree an hour, about its own width; the star, angles and times in this table, which are an example, not real data; and timing the moons of Jupiter as another astronomical method, which worked on land but not on a rolling ship: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

The horological method: Harrison's marine chronometer

The simplest answer is a clock that keeps Greenwich time all voyage: the horological method (horology is clockmaking). Ordinary clocks failed at sea: a ship rolls, and changes of temperature and damp make them gain or lose. Every 4 minutes a clock is out puts the ship 1° wrong. John Harrison built a series of sea clocks, ending with a large watch, H4, that kept time well enough to give longitude to within the prize's limit. With one on board, longitude is just local noon against the chronometer.

The longitude prize

Not to scale.

Ships lost at sea pushed Britain to offer a large prize for a practical method. Two methods competed. Astronomers, led by the Astronomer Royal Nevil Maskelyne, backed lunar distances and published the tables sailors needed in the Nautical Almanac. John Harrison, a clockmaker, spent decades on his sea clocks. Both worked: lunar distances were cheaper for years, until chronometers became affordable and took over.

The Longitude Act of 1714 and its prize of £20,000; Nevil Maskelyne, Astronomer Royal, and the first Nautical Almanac (for 1767); John Harrison, H4 and its 1761 trial voyage to Jamaica; and Harrison being paid in 1773; ships lost at sea driving the prize; and lunar distances staying cheaper until chronometers became affordable: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.
