Galilean refractor

A diverging (concave) eyepiece lens, giving an upright image but a narrow field of view — what Galileo himself used in 1609.

Keplerian refractor

A converging (convex) eyepiece lens, giving a wider field of view but an inverted image — the layout almost all modern refractors use.

Newtonian reflector

A parabolic primary mirror reflects light back up to a flat secondary mirror, out to an eyepiece on the side of the tube.

Cassegrain reflector

A parabolic primary mirror reflects light up to a convex secondary, back down through a hole in the primary to an eyepiece behind it.

  • No chromatic aberration. Reflection doesn't depend on wavelength, so there's no colour fringing to correct for.
  • Much larger apertures. A mirror can be supported across its whole back; a lens can only be held at its rim.
  • A long focal length in a short tube. Folding the light path back on itself — most dramatically in a Cassegrain — reaches a long effective focal length without a correspondingly long tube.
  • Multiple mirrors can be combined. Several smaller segments can act together as one much larger effective aperture.

Galileo's own telescope was a small Galilean refractor, with none of these advantages — yet it was still enough to see Jupiter's four largest moons and the phases of Venus for the first time.

Astronomy doesn't mind an inverted image — there's no "upright" in space to compare it against — so Keplerian's wider field and brighter view win out for telescopes; a Galilean upright image matters more on the ground, where things really do have a right way up.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: magnification (fobjective / feyepiece, or the effective focal length in its place for a folded reflector) depends only on the two focal lengths; light grasp and resolution depend only on the objective's diameter. Swapping in a shorter eyepiece raises magnification without improving resolution at all — it just magnifies the blur. The telescope bench ↑

Common exam confusion: "resolution improves" means the resolvable angle gets smaller — a telescope that can separate two objects only 1 arcsecond apart has better resolution than one that needs 2 arcseconds, even though 1 is the smaller number. Resolving power and light grasp ↑

Common exam link: don't mix up the four designs' key distinguishing features: Galilean (diverging eyepiece lens, upright image), Keplerian (converging eyepiece lens, inverted image), Newtonian (flat secondary mirror, eyepiece on the side), Cassegrain (convex secondary mirror, eyepiece behind a hole in the primary).
