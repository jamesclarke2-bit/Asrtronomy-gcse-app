Telescopes: Magnification, Light Grasp and Resolution

Why a telescope sees more than your eye does, the three numbers that describe how well it does it, the four classic designs behind nearly every telescope ever built, and why reflectors took over from refractors for anything serious.

Why telescopes

The human eye is itself a small optical instrument, and a surprisingly capable one — but it has two hard limits a telescope exists to beat. Its — the pupil — opens to only about 7 mm across even fully dark-adapted, which caps both how much light it can gather and how fine a detail it can resolve. And unlike a camera, it can't build up a longer exposure: a faint object either produces enough signal to register in under a second, or it simply never becomes visible, no matter how long you stare.

A telescope's objective — its main lens or mirror — is simply a much larger aperture, feeding a magnified, brighter image to the eye behind the eyepiece. Everything below follows from that one idea: a bigger aperture gathers more light and resolves finer detail, and the ratio of two lenses' focal lengths sets how large the image appears.

Magnification, light grasp and resolution

Three numbers describe what a telescope actually does, and the exam gives you exact formulas for all three. Magnification depends only on the two :

magnification = fobjective / feyepiece

and resolution, by contrast, depend only on the objective's diameter — its aperture — not on either focal length. Light grasp is proportional to the square of the diameter, so doubling the aperture gives four times the light grasp:

light grasp ∝ diameter²

improves with a larger objective diameter and gets worse at longer wavelengths. The Rayleigh criterion gives the actual angle a telescope can just resolve:

resolution (radians) = 1.22 × wavelength / diameter

Four telescope designs

Every telescope built since Galileo's time is some variation on four classic designs — two refracting (using a lens as the objective), two reflecting (using a mirror). Reflectors vs refractors

A refracting lens bends different wavelengths of light by slightly different amounts, so they don't all focus at exactly the same point — the colour fringing around bright objects called . A mirror reflects every wavelength at the same angle, so a reflecting telescope has none of it at all — one of several real advantages reflectors have over refractors:

  • No chromatic aberration. Reflection doesn't depend on wavelength, so there's no colour fringing to correct for.
  • Much larger apertures. A mirror can be supported across its whole back; a lens can only be held at its rim, which limits how large (and how heavy) it can practically be made before it sags under its own weight.
  • A long focal length in a short tube. Folding the light path back on itself — most dramatically in a Cassegrain — reaches a long effective focal length without needing a correspondingly long tube.
  • Multiple mirrors can be combined. Several smaller mirror segments can act together as one much larger effective aperture, which isn't practical with a single lens.

Galileo's own telescope was a small Galilean refractor, with none of these advantages — yet it was still enough to see Jupiter's four largest moons and the phases of Venus for the first time. See Galileo's telescopic evidence for what those two observations showed.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: magnification (fobjective / feyepiece) depends only on the two focal lengths; light grasp and resolution depend only on the objective's diameter. Swapping in a shorter eyepiece raises magnification without improving resolution at all — it just magnifies the blur. Magnification, light grasp and resolution ↑

Common exam confusion: "resolution improves" means the resolvable angle gets smaller — a telescope that can separate two objects only 1 arcsecond apart has better resolution than one that needs 2 arcseconds, even though 1 is the smaller number. Magnification, light grasp and resolution ↑

Common exam link: don't mix up the four designs' key distinguishing features: Galilean (diverging eyepiece lens, upright image), Keplerian (converging eyepiece lens, inverted image), Newtonian (flat secondary mirror, eyepiece on the side), Cassegrain (convex secondary mirror, eyepiece behind a hole in the primary).
