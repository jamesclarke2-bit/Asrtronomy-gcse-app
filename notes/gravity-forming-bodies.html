Gravity and forming bodies

How to spot gravity at work in the Solar System, and three things gravity decides for every planet and moon: whether it survives close in, whether it is round, and whether it keeps air.

Regular, predictable motion

Not to scale. Planet periods from the data sheet.

Gravity from the Sun keeps every planet going round in the same path, in the same time, orbit after orbit; the Earth's gravity does the same for the Moon. Because the motion is so regular, it can be predicted: the planets' positions, the Moon's phases, and eclipses are worked out years ahead. Kepler's laws describe exactly how (see Kepler's laws). If you see something that repeats like clockwork, you are seeing gravity's regular motion.

The Moon's phase cycle of about 29.5 days: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Several bodies at once: Lagrange points

Not to scale: L1 and L2 are drawn much further from the Earth than they really are.

When two big bodies orbit together, like the Sun and the Earth, their gravity combines. There are five places, called Lagrange points (L1 to L5), where a small object can go round the Sun in step with the Earth, keeping the same position compared with both. Space telescopes are parked near L2, the far orbit on the Space telescopes page, where the Sun, the Earth and the Moon all stay on one side. Dust and small asteroids gather near L4 and L5.

L1 and L2 about 1.5 million km from the Earth, telescopes parked near L2, and dust and asteroids gathering at L4 and L5 (Jupiter's Trojan asteroids are an example): first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Resonances, shifting orbits and chaos

A resonance is when orbit times fit a simple whole-number pattern. Io, Europa and Ganymede go round Jupiter in a 1 : 2 : 4 pattern, so the same tugs between them repeat at the same places, again and again; this keeps Io's orbit oval and heats it (see tidal heating). Shifting orbits: pulls between planets can move orbits over time; the giant planets are thought to have moved after they formed (see formation theories). Chaos: tiny pulls add up, so exact positions millions of years ahead cannot be predicted.

The 1 : 2 : 4 pattern is drawn exactly; the real periods are about 1.8, 3.6 and 7.2 days, very close to it. Io and Ganymede are drawn starting together with Europa opposite, as in the real pattern, which never lines all three up on one side. The giant planets moving, and chaos over millions of years: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Too close: the Roche limit

A planet pulls harder on the near side of a moon than on its far side, stretching it: a tidal force. Far out, the moon's own gravity holds it together. Inside the Roche limit, the stretch wins and the moon is pulled apart; the pieces spread into a ring. It also stops a moon forming that close: the pieces cannot gather into one body. That is why rings sit close to their planets (see ring systems).

The Roche limit of about 2.5 planet radii (for a moon about as dense as its planet), and Saturn's main rings lying inside its Roche limit: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Round or lumpy?

Every part of a body pulls on every other part. In a small body that pull is weak, and solid rock is strong enough to keep any lumpy shape. In a big body, gravity is strong enough to squash the rock and pull it into the shape where every part of the surface is as close to the centre as it can be: a sphere. So big bodies are round and small ones are lumpy.

Bodies becoming round from about 400 km across (icy) to about 600 km (rocky), and the sizes of Phobos (about 22 km), Vesta (about 525 km, not quite round) and Ceres (about 940 km, round): first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Holding on to an atmosphere

Gas particles move faster the hotter and lighter the gas is. A particle moving faster than the body's escape speed (the speed needed to escape its gravity; see Space probes) can leave. A body keeps a gas only if its escape speed is well above the particles' typical speed.

The rule that a gas stays for billions of years if the escape speed is more than 6 times its particles' typical speed, the escape speeds (Jupiter about 59.5 km/s, Earth 11.2, Mars 5.0, Titan 2.6, the Moon 2.4) and the temperatures: first draft, to be checked by the teacher. These are upper-atmosphere temperatures, where gas escapes, not the ground's: Jupiter and Earth about 1,000 K, Mars 250 K, Titan 150 K; the Moon, with almost no air, uses its sunlit surface, 390 K.
