Radio telescopes

A radio telescope is a giant curved dish that collects radio waves from space instead of light.

How a simple radio telescope works

The dish focuses radio waves in the same way that a curved mirror focuses light. Follow steps 1 to 4.

  1. The dish collects radio waves and reflects them to one point, its focus.
  2. A receiver at the focus turns the radio waves into a tiny electrical signal.
  3. An amplifier makes this very weak signal stronger.
  4. A computer records how strong the signal is.

A dish measures the signal from one small patch of sky at a time. To make a picture, it scans across the sky point by point, and the computer shades each point by its signal strength: brighter for a stronger signal.

Many dishes working as one

Several dishes spread over many kilometres can be linked. A computer combines their signals, timed exactly, so together they pick out detail like a single dish as wide as the farthest-apart pair. This is an aperture synthesis array.

Each pair of dishes fills in part of that giant dish. As the Earth turns, each pair swings round and fills in more of it.

What radio astronomy found

  • Quasars: found as strong radio sources that look like faint stars; they are the bright centres of very distant galaxies.
  • Jets from black holes: radio maps show narrow jets shooting out from black holes at the centres of some galaxies.
  • The Milky Way's structure: radio waves from hydrogen gas pass through the dust that hides much of our Galaxy, so radio maps traced its spiral arms.
  • Protoplanetary discs: radio arrays have pictured discs of dust and gas around young stars, with gaps where planets may be forming.
