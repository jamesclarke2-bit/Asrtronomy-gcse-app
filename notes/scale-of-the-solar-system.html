The Scale of the Solar System

From the Moon, a short hop away, out through the planets to the nearest stars — plain kilometres stop being a sensible way to write any of these distances down well before you reach the edge of the Solar System. This page covers the scale itself, the astronomical unit, light year and parsec that exist to deal with it, and how long light takes to cross it.

The eight planets bunch up on this scale, Mercury to Neptune spanning under two powers of ten, while the nearest star is over three more beyond: farther than Neptune is from the Sun. That gap is why the Solar System has its own unit (the AU) and interstellar space uses light years and parsecs.

Units of astronomical distance

Three specialist units of distance appear throughout astronomy, each sized to keep the numbers at its own scale sensible rather than awkwardly huge or tiny.

Unit Value Used for
Distances within the Solar System — planets, moons, comets and asteroids. Neptune sits at a manageable 30 AU, rather than 4.5 billion km.
Distances to stars and galaxies, especially in popular writing — intuitive, since it directly tells you how long the light you're seeing has been travelling.
The unit professional astronomers prefer for stellar and galactic distances — defined directly from how a star's position appears to shift (parallax) as Earth orbits the Sun, which is how many of these distances are actually measured.

The AU, the Sun-Earth distance, was first measured precisely by timing transits of Venus (Venus crossing the Sun's face) from widely separated places on Earth: see Transits of Venus and measuring the AU.

See Parallax and the Parsec for exactly how that stellar shift gives a star's distance, and why the parsec is defined the way it is.

Light travel time

Light's speed is on the data sheet, so time = distance / speed turns each distance into how long light takes to reach each planet from the Sun: a feel for the Solar System's scale, like the delay on a very long-distance call.

Body Distance Light travel time

The Moon's row is measured from Earth; every other row is sunlight reaching that planet. This is why the unit is called a light year: the nearest star beyond the Sun is about 4.25 light years away, so its light takes about 4.25 years to reach us.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the data sheet's planet distances are each measured from the Sun, not from Earth. Mars's distance from Earth actually varies between roughly 0.5 AU and 2.5 AU depending on where both planets currently sit on their own orbits — the data sheet's single 1.5 AU figure is Mars's distance from the Sun, not from us. Common exam confusion: a parsec is bigger than a light year (1 pc = 3.26 ly), not the other way round — easy to mix up since "parsec" doesn't sound bigger. The order, smallest to largest, is AU, then light year, then parsec. Units of astronomical distance ↑

Common exam link: light travel time means you never see a distant object as it is now — only as it was however long ago its light set out. Even the Sun you see is about 8 minutes in the past; Saturn's light takes well over an hour. Light travel time ↑
