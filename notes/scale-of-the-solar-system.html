The Scale of the Solar System

From the Moon, a short hop away, out through the planets to the nearest stars — plain kilometres stop being a sensible way to write any of these distances down well before you reach the edge of the Solar System. This page covers the scale itself, the astronomical unit, light year and parsec that exist to deal with it, and how long light takes to cross it.

Distances across the Solar System

Drag the slider below across a logarithmic scale — each step multiplies the distance rather than adding to it, the only sensible way to fit the Moon and the nearest star on the same line. The planets are placed using the mean distances from the Sun in AU on the exam data sheet (Formulae and Data Sheet), converted to km.

Notice how tightly the eight planets bunch up together on this scale — Mercury to Neptune spans less than two decades (powers of ten) — while the gap from Neptune to the nearest star beyond the Sun is over three more decades on top of that: more than 10,000× farther than Neptune is from the Sun. That gap is exactly why the Solar System gets its own unit (the AU) and interstellar space gets different ones (light years and parsecs) — see the next section.

Units of astronomical distance

Three specialist units of distance appear throughout astronomy, each sized to keep the numbers at its own scale sensible rather than awkwardly huge or tiny.

Unit Value Used for
Distances within the Solar System — planets, moons, comets and asteroids. Neptune sits at a manageable 30 AU, rather than 4.5 billion km.
Distances to stars and galaxies, especially in popular writing — intuitive, since it directly tells you how long the light you're seeing has been travelling.
The unit professional astronomers prefer for stellar and galactic distances — defined directly from how a star's position appears to shift (parallax) as Earth orbits the Sun, which is how many of these distances are actually measured.

The AU itself — the Sun-Earth distance — was first measured precisely using transits of Venus: Venus passing directly across the face of the Sun, as seen from Earth. Timing that transit from several widely separated places on Earth let astronomers triangulate the Sun's true distance. That method, and why a transit of Venus is rare, is covered in Topic 11's own notes.

Light travel time

Distance and travel time are two sides of the same coin once you fix a speed, and light's speed (on the data sheet) is about as fixed as speeds get. The table below uses time = distance / speed to show how long light takes to reach each planet from the Sun — a genuinely intuitive way to feel the Solar System's scale, since it's the same delay you'd see in a (very long-distance) phone call.

This is exactly why the unit is called a light year: the nearest star beyond the Sun sits about 4.25 light years away, and — by that same definition — its light takes about 4.25 years to reach us.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the data sheet's planet distances are each measured from the Sun, not from Earth. Mars's distance from Earth actually varies between roughly 0.5 AU and 2.5 AU depending on where both planets currently sit on their own orbits — the data sheet's single 1.5 AU figure is Mars's distance from the Sun, not from us.
