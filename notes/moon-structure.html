The Moon: Structure, Surface and Origin

What the Moon is made of and what its surface looks like, how to recognise its named features, why we only ever see one side of it, how we've seen the other side anyway, what it takes to get there — and where the Moon came from.

Shape and size

The Moon is a near-sphere, with a mean diameter of about 3,500 km (3,475 km more precisely).

Inside the Moon

Like Earth, the Moon is layered into a crust, a mantle and a core — but in very different proportions.

The Moon's layers compared with Earth's

The ~2 km offset shows up as the Moon's centre of mass sitting about 2 km closer to Earth than its geometric centre.

Surface features and how they formed

Craters

Bowl-shaped hollows made by meteoroid impacts, ranging from tiny pits to basins hundreds of kilometres across, and varying widely in age. With no air to burn up incoming rock and almost no erosion, craters last for billions of years.

Maria (“seas”)

Dark grey, relatively smooth plains. They're not water: they formed when ancient lava welled up and flooded low-lying impact basins, then set hard.

Terrae (highlands)

Light grey, mountainous and rugged. The highlands are older than the maria and far more heavily cratered, because they've been collecting impacts for longer.

Mountains

Ranges such as the Apennines are the uplifted rims of huge impact basins — thrown up by the impact itself, not by volcanic activity.

Valleys and rilles

Narrow, channel-like depressions winding across the maria, thought to be collapsed lava tubes or channels left behind by ancient lava flows.

The far side looks different: it's far more heavily cratered and has almost no maria. That's not mainly because it was hit more often — on the near side, lava floods buried and erased many older craters, while the far side's thicker crust kept most lava from reaching the surface, so its ancient craters survive.

Synchronous rotation: why we only see one side

The Moon does spin on its axis — it's a common misconception that it doesn't. It turns exactly once for every orbit of Earth, so its rotation period and orbital period are equal, both about 27.3 days: the sidereal month. Because the two keep pace, the same face always points towards Earth.

Libration: seeing slightly more than half

Even though the same face always points our way, the Moon appears to rock and nod slightly, letting us peek a little way round its edges. Over time this lets us see about 59% of its surface from Earth — though never the remaining 41%. It has three separate causes:

Libration in longitude

The Moon spins at a perfectly steady rate, but its orbit is an ellipse, so it moves faster near perigee and slower near apogee (see perigee and apogee). The steady spin and the uneven orbit drift slightly out of step, so we see up to about 8° further round its eastern and western edges.

Libration in latitude

The Moon's axis is tilted about 6.7° to the plane of its orbit, so during each orbit we see a little way over its north pole, then a little way under its south pole — like seeing the top and then the bottom of a slowly nodding head.

Diurnal libration

A small parallax effect from where you stand: Earth's rotation carries you across its ~12,700 km width between moonrise and moonset, so you view the Moon from slightly different angles — about 1° further round one edge, then the other.

Near side and far side

Because of synchronous rotation, the side facing Earth is always the same, and the two hemispheres look strikingly different. The near side is marked by large dark maria; the far side is almost devoid of them.

The structural cause is the crust. It is thickest on the far side — up to about 60 km in the central far-side highlands, against an average of 34–43 km — and thinnest, close to zero, beneath some large impact basins such as the Sea of Crises (GRAIL; Wieczorek et al. 2013, cited above). Molten rock rising from inside the Moon could break through the thinner near-side crust and flood its low-lying impact basins, but mostly couldn't get through the far side's. That is also why the far side keeps so many more of its ancient craters, as noted under surface features above.

Seeing the far side

Nobody on Earth has ever seen the far side directly, so everything we know about it comes from spacecraft:

  • 1959, Luna 3 (Soviet Union) — the first photographs of the far side, blurry by modern standards, but enough to show it had almost no maria.
  • 1966–67, Lunar Orbiter program (USA) — five orbiters mapped almost the whole Moon, far side included, in far more detail.
  • 1968–72, Apollo — astronauts photographed the far side from lunar orbit; the Apollo 8 crew were the first people to see it with their own eyes.
  • 2009 onwards, the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and other modern missions — detailed global maps and surface-height measurements of the whole Moon.

Getting there: escape velocity and rockets

is the speed something needs to coast away from a planet's gravity without any further push. For Earth it's about 11.2 km/s — over 40,000 km/h. A spacecraft heading for the Moon has to be accelerated to nearly this speed. It needs slightly less than the full amount, because the Moon itself is still held by Earth's gravity.

Only rocket propulsion can supply that much energy, for these reasons:

  • The speed has to be built up by pushing continuously for several minutes, first through the thin upper atmosphere and then through the vacuum of space.
  • Jet engines and propellers work by pushing on air and by burning fuel with oxygen from the air — and space has neither.
  • A rocket carries its own oxidiser as well as its fuel, and pushes itself forward by throwing hot exhaust gas out backwards very fast. It needs nothing to push against, so it keeps working in a vacuum.

The actual routes spacecraft take to the Moon are covered later, with space probes.

Where did the Moon come from?

The leading explanation is the . About 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized body called Theia struck the young Earth a glancing blow. Theia was vaporised, along with part of Earth's mantle, and the debris thrown into orbit around Earth gathered together to form the Moon.

The evidence for it:

  • Oxygen isotopes: the ratios of oxygen isotopes in Moon rocks are almost identical to Earth's, so the two formed from closely related material.
  • Missing water and volatiles: lunar samples have very little water or other easily evaporated substances, which would have boiled away in the heat of the impact.
  • KREEP: rocks rich in potassium (K), rare-earth elements (REE) and phosphorus (P) are found in the Ocean of Storms and the Sea of Showers (Mare Imbrium, also called the Sea of Rains). KREEP is what's left over when a huge ocean of molten rock cools and crystallises — and the impact's heat would have melted the young Moon's outer layers into exactly such an ocean.

There are two named alternatives:

Capture Theory

The Moon formed somewhere else in the Solar System and was later captured by Earth's gravity. But capturing a body this large needs very special conditions, and a Moon from elsewhere wouldn't be expected to match Earth's oxygen isotopes so closely.

Co-accretion Theory

Earth and the Moon formed together, side by side, from the same cloud of material. But that would give the Moon a composition much like Earth's, including its water and volatiles and a large iron core — and the Moon has very few volatiles and only a small core.
