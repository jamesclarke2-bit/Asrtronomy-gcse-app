The Moon: Surface and Rotation

What the Moon is shaped like, what its surface features are and how they formed, how to recognise the named ones on sight — and why we only ever see one side of it.

Shape and size

The Moon is a near-sphere, with a mean diameter of about 3,500 km (3,475 km more precisely).

Surface features and how they formed

Craters

Bowl-shaped hollows made by meteoroid impacts, ranging from tiny pits to basins hundreds of kilometres across, and varying widely in age. With no air to burn up incoming rock and almost no erosion, craters last for billions of years.

Maria (“seas”)

Dark grey, relatively smooth plains. They're not water: they formed when ancient lava welled up and flooded low-lying impact basins, then set hard.

Terrae (highlands)

Light grey, mountainous and rugged. The highlands are older than the maria and far more heavily cratered, because they've been collecting impacts for longer.

Mountains

Ranges such as the Apennines are the uplifted rims of huge impact basins — thrown up by the impact itself, not by volcanic activity.

Valleys and rilles

Narrow, channel-like depressions winding across the maria, thought to be collapsed lava tubes or channels left behind by ancient lava flows.

The far side looks different: it's far more heavily cratered and has almost no maria. That's not mainly because it was hit more often — on the near side, lava floods buried and erased many older craters, while the far side's thicker crust kept most lava from reaching the surface, so its ancient craters survive.

Synchronous rotation: why we only see one side

The Moon does spin on its axis — it's a common misconception that it doesn't. It turns exactly once for every orbit of Earth, so its rotation period and orbital period are equal, both about 27.3 days: the sidereal month. Because the two keep pace, the same face always points towards Earth.

Rotating once per orbit: the red spot always faces Earth

What actually happens

One spin per orbit keeps the same hemisphere turned to us all month.

Not rotating: the red spot keeps pointing the same way

If it didn't rotate at all

We'd see every side of the Moon over the course of a month.

Libration: seeing slightly more than half

Even though the same face always points our way, the Moon appears to rock and nod slightly, letting us peek a little way round its edges. Over time this lets us see about 59% of its surface from Earth — though never the remaining 41%. It has three separate causes:

Libration in longitude

The Moon spins at a perfectly steady rate, but its orbit is an ellipse, so it moves faster near perigee and slower near apogee (see perigee and apogee). The steady spin and the uneven orbit drift slightly out of step, so we see up to about 8° further round its eastern and western edges.

Libration in latitude

The Moon's axis is tilted about 6.7° to the plane of its orbit, so during each orbit we see a little way over its north pole, then a little way under its south pole — like seeing the top and then the bottom of a slowly nodding head.

Diurnal libration

A small parallax effect from where you stand: Earth's rotation carries you across its ~12,700 km width between moonrise and moonset, so you view the Moon from slightly different angles — about 1° further round one edge, then the other.
