The Moon: Structure, Surface and Origin

What the Moon is made of and what its surface looks like, how to recognise its named features, why we only ever see one side of it, how we've seen the other side anyway, what it takes to get there — and where the Moon came from.

Inside the Moon

The Moon is a near-sphere about 3,500 km across, layered like Earth into a crust, mantle and core — but in very different proportions. Tap a layer for more.

  • Crust — thin and rocky, ~34–43 km thick on average.
  • Mantle — most of the Moon's interior, semi-solid rock.
  • Outer core — a thin, partly molten layer around the inner core.
  • Inner core — small, dense and likely solid.

Surface features and how they formed

Craters

Impact scars, from tiny pits to huge basins. With no air or erosion, they last for billions of years.

Maria (“seas”)

Dark, smooth plains — not water, but ancient lava that flooded impact basins and set hard.

Terrae (highlands)

Light, rugged ground. Older than the maria, so far more heavily cratered.

Mountains

Ranges like the Apennines are impact-basin rims, thrown up by the impact itself, not volcanoes.

Valleys and rilles

Channel-like dips across the maria, thought to be collapsed lava tubes or old lava channels.

Synchronous rotation: why we only see one side

The Moon does spin on its axis. It turns exactly once for every orbit of Earth, so its rotation period and orbital period are equal, both about 27.3 days: the sidereal month. Because the two keep pace, the same face always points towards Earth.

Libration: seeing slightly more than half

Even though the same face always points our way, the Moon appears to rock and nod slightly, letting us peek a little way round its edges — a . Three diagrams, three causes.

Libration in longitude: the steady spin against the uneven orbit

Top-down, hugely exaggerated. The Moon turns at a steady rate (blue marker), but moves faster near perigee and slower near apogee (Kepler's second law), so the true Earth-facing point (red marker) swings a little east, then west, of it.

Libration in latitude: the tilted axis

Side-on, hugely exaggerated. The Moon's axis stays tilted about 6.7° the same way in space all orbit, so Earth's view nods: a little over the north pole on one side of the orbit, a little under the south pole on the other.

Near side and far side

Because of synchronous rotation, the side facing Earth is always the same — and the two hemispheres look strikingly different.

There's a far side, but no permanently “dark side”: both sides get day and night, since the Moon rotates once per orbit.

·

Still an open question — the “lunar far-side highlands problem”. One hypothesis, put forward in 2025 and not yet tested: once the Moon became tidally locked, Earth's own heat kept the near side very hot, close to 1,000°C, while the far side, facing away, cooled to around −20°C. That split may have set up a circulation in the Moon's early magma ocean, piling light, floating plagioclase crystals into a thicker crust on the far side. A hypothesis, not an established fact — first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Further reading: Lewis Dartnell, “Why does the Moon's far side look so different to the near side?”, BBC Sky at Night Magazine (22 December 2025).

Seeing the far side

Nobody on Earth has ever seen the far side directly, so everything we know about it comes from spacecraft:

  • 1959, Luna 3 (Soviet Union) — the first photographs of the far side, blurry by modern standards, but enough to show it had almost no maria.
  • 1966–67, Lunar Orbiter program (USA) — five orbiters mapped almost the whole Moon, far side included, in far more detail.
  • 1968–72, Apollo — astronauts photographed the far side from lunar orbit; the Apollo 8 crew were the first people to see it with their own eyes.
  • 2009 onwards, the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) and other modern missions — detailed global maps and surface-height measurements of the whole Moon.

Getting there: escape velocity and rockets

is the speed something needs to coast away from a planet's gravity without any further push. For Earth it's about 11.2 km/s — over 40,000 km/h. A spacecraft heading for the Moon has to be accelerated to nearly this speed. It needs slightly less than the full amount, because the Moon itself is still held by Earth's gravity.

Only rocket propulsion can supply that much energy, for these reasons:

  • The speed has to be built up by pushing continuously for several minutes, first through the thin upper atmosphere and then through the vacuum of space.
  • Jet engines and propellers work by pushing on air and by burning fuel with oxygen from the air — and space has neither.
  • A rocket carries its own oxidiser as well as its fuel, and pushes itself forward by throwing hot exhaust gas out backwards very fast. It needs nothing to push against, so it keeps working in a vacuum.

The actual routes spacecraft take to the Moon are covered later, with space probes.

Where did the Moon come from?

The leading explanation is the . About 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized body called Theia struck the young Earth a glancing blow. Theia was vaporised, along with part of Earth's mantle, and the debris thrown into orbit around Earth gathered together to form the Moon.

The evidence for it:

  • Oxygen isotopes: the ratios of oxygen isotopes in Moon rocks are almost identical to Earth's, so the two formed from closely related material.
  • Missing water and volatiles: lunar samples have very little water or other easily evaporated substances, which would have boiled away in the heat of the impact.
  • KREEP: rocks rich in potassium (K), rare-earth elements (REE) and phosphorus (P) are found in the Ocean of Storms and the Sea of Showers (Mare Imbrium, also called the Sea of Rains). KREEP is what's left over when a huge ocean of molten rock cools and crystallises — and the impact's heat would have melted the young Moon's outer layers into exactly such an ocean.

There are two named alternatives:

Capture Theory

The Moon formed somewhere else in the Solar System and was later captured by Earth's gravity. But capturing a body this large needs very special conditions, and a Moon from elsewhere wouldn't be expected to match Earth's oxygen isotopes so closely.

Co-accretion Theory

Earth and the Moon formed together, side by side, from the same cloud of material. But that would give the Moon a composition much like Earth's, including its water and volatiles and a large iron core — and the Moon has very few volatiles and only a small core.
