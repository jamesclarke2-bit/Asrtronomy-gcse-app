Measuring the Sky

How ancient astronomers measured the size of the Earth and the sizes and distances of the Moon and Sun with nothing but shadows and angles, what the real figures are, and why the Pole Star won't be the Pole Star forever.

Eratosthenes measures the Earth

Around 240 BCE, Eratosthenes, a scholar in Alexandria, heard that at noon on the summer solstice the Sun shone straight down a deep well at Syene (modern Aswan), far to the south. The Sun was at the there, so upright objects cast no shadow. At the same moment in Alexandria, a vertical stick, a , did cast a shadow: the Sun was about 7.2° away from overhead, one fiftieth of a full circle.

The Sun is so far away that its rays arrive at Earth effectively parallel. So the only reason the angle differs between the two cities is that Earth's surface curves between them, and the 7.2° shadow angle is also the angle between the two cities at Earth's centre.

The shadow angle at Alexandria equals the angle between the two cities at Earth's centre

The angle is drawn three times bigger than the real 7.2° so it's visible.

If 7.2° is one fiftieth of a circle, the distance from Syene to Alexandria must be one fiftieth of Earth's whole circumference:

angle ÷ 360° = distance between the places ÷ circumference

Eratosthenes took the distance to be 5,000 stadia, giving 250,000 stadia. Nobody knows exactly which length of stadion he meant; depending on which, that's somewhere between about 39,000 and 46,000 km. With today's figure of about 800 km between the two cities, 7.2° gives 40,000 km, almost exactly the real circumference.

It only works if the two measurements are taken at local noon on the same day, at places due north and south of each other (Eratosthenes assumed Syene was due south of Alexandria; it's actually about 3° of longitude east).

The same geometry as the meridian cross-section

This isn't a new idea: it's the geometry behind the meridian cross-section on the Star Coordinates page. There, an object's altitude as it crosses the meridian is 90° − |latitude − declination|. For the same object at the same moment, moving north or south changes its noon altitude by exactly the change in latitude. Eratosthenes' object was the Sun, and his shadow angle was that change. To see it for yourself, use the meridian cross-section on Star Coordinates:

  1. Set the declination to +23.4°, where the Sun sits at the June solstice.
  2. Set the latitude to 24°, Syene: the transit altitude is almost 90°, straight overhead.
  3. Move the latitude to 31°, Alexandria: the altitude drops by 7°, which is Eratosthenes' shadow angle.

Aristarchus: the sizes and distances of the Moon and Sun

A few decades earlier, Aristarchus of Samos found ways to compare the Moon and Sun with the Earth using only observations of the sky. His methods were sound, though some of his measurements were not.

The Moon's size, from a lunar eclipse

During a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through Earth's , and the curved edge of the shadow shows how big it is compared with the Moon. Timing how long the Moon takes to cross the shadow, compared with how long it takes to move its own width, gives the same answer: the shadow is about Moons wide.

Earth's shadow at the distance of the Moon, drawn to scale with the Moon

Earth itself is a little bigger than its shadow, because the Sun is bigger than Earth and the shadow narrows as it goes. Between Earth and the Moon it narrows by about Moon's width, so Earth is about Moons wide, and the Moon is roughly of Earth's diameter: around 3,500 km. (Aristarchus himself estimated a little over a third.)

Once the Moon's size is known, its distance follows from how big it looks: it covers about half a degree of sky, which puts it roughly 110 of its own diameters away, about 380,000 km.

The Sun's distance, from the quarter Moon

At first quarter, the Moon is exactly half lit. That can only happen when the angle Sun–Moon–Earth is a right angle. Aristarchus measured the angle between the Moon and the Sun as seen from Earth, θ, and used the right-angled triangle to work out how much farther away the Sun is than the Moon: cos θ = Earth–Moon distance ÷ Earth–Sun distance.

Not to scale: the real angle is so close to 90° that the real triangle is hundreds of times longer than it is tall.

Aristarchus measured 87°, making the Sun about 19 times farther away than the Moon. The true angle is , which makes it about times farther. The method was right; the angle is just too close to 90° to measure by eye. Even so, since the Sun and Moon look the same size, a Sun 19 times farther away had to be 19 times wider than the Moon, and so far bigger than Earth. That led Aristarchus to suggest that the Earth goes around the Sun, nearly 1,800 years before Copernicus.

The 400× coincidence

The Sun is about times the Moon's diameter, and also about times farther away. The two factors almost cancel, so from Earth the Sun and Moon have almost exactly the same , about half a degree ( for the Moon at its mean distance, for the Sun). That's why the Moon can just cover the Sun in a total solar eclipse. The Moon's distance varies, so when it's farther away than average it looks slightly smaller than the Sun, and an eclipse is annular instead.

Precession: the wandering pole

Earth spins like a slightly lopsided top. The Sun's and Moon's gravity pull on its equatorial bulge and try to straighten its tilt. Instead of straightening, the spinning Earth responds by slowly swinging the direction of its axis around in a circle. This is . The tilt stays about 23.4°, but the direction the axis points in space traces out a cone, taking about 26,000 years to go once round.

On the sky, the north celestial pole therefore traces a circle about 23.4° in radius around the . Whichever bright star happens to lie near the pole at the time is the "pole star", so Polaris hasn't always been the pole star, and won't always be.

As seen looking up at the northern sky. Star positions are today's; the stars' own motions over these timescales are small enough to leave out, and the pole's circle is treated as perfectly steady, so dates are good to within a few centuries.

Precession also slowly shifts every star's right ascension and declination, which is why star charts and catalogues state the date their coordinates are for (such as J2000), and it moves the equinoxes westward along the ecliptic by about 1° every 72 years.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: Eratosthenes' 7.2° is the difference between the Sun's angles at the two places. It only equals the angle at Earth's centre because the Sun's rays arrive parallel, and only if both readings are taken at local noon on the same day, at places due north and south of each other.
