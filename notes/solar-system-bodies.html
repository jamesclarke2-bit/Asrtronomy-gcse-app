Ring systems

All four giant planets have rings — but only one of them is bright enough to notice.

  • Jupiter: a very faint, thin ring of dark dusty particles, found only by spacecraft.
  • Saturn: by far the brightest and widest rings in the Solar System, easily seen from Earth.
  • Uranus: narrow, dark rings, tilted up almost side-on along with the planet itself.
  • Neptune: faint rings made of clumpy arcs rather than one smooth band.

Tidal heating

Io's orbit around Jupiter isn't quite circular — kept that way by a gravitational tug-of-war with two neighbouring moons.

Exaggerated for clarity — Io's real eccentricity is only about 0.004. Closer to Jupiter means a stronger pull on the near side than the far side, flexing Io's interior; that flexing heats it by friction, driving Io's volcanoes.

The tick on the slider marks Io's real value. At that value the orbit's own path is almost a perfect circle — but the diagram still exaggerates how much Io's own shape flexes, otherwise the flexing would be far too small to see at all.

What makes a dwarf planet?

Three questions, asked in order, give the IAU's own dividing line.

Does it orbit the Sun?

No → a moon, or a small Solar System body

Is it nearly round (in hydrostatic equilibrium)?

No → a moon, or a small Solar System body

Has it cleared its orbit of other debris?

No → dwarf planet

Yes to all three → planet

E.g. Ceres and Pluto: round, but share their orbit with other debris — dwarf planets, not planets.

Asteroids, meteoroids and comets

Beyond the planets and dwarf planets, the Solar System holds huge numbers of much smaller bodies, in three broad categories:

Asteroids

Small, rocky (or metallic) bodies, mostly orbiting the Sun in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter. Ceres, in the table above, is the largest.

Meteoroids

Much smaller fragments of rock or dust in space — often broken off an asteroid, or shed by a comet. See Meteoroids and meteorites below for what happens when one reaches Earth.

Comets

Small bodies of ice, dust and rock that develop a glowing coma and tail(s) as they near the Sun. See Comets below.

Short-period and long-period comets

Short-period comets (orbital period under roughly 200 years, e.g. Halley's and Encke's Comets) are thought to come from the , a disc of icy bodies beyond Neptune lying roughly in the same plane as the planets. Meteoroids, meteorites and the ecliptic plane

A meteoroid that survives its fall through the atmosphere and reaches the ground is a meteorite — a different object from a meteor, the streak of light you see while it's still burning up (see Observing Techniques for meteor showers, caused by Earth passing through a comet's debris trail). Meteorites are grouped by what they're made of: stony (the most common, similar to asteroid rock), iron (dense and metallic, from the shattered cores of differentiated asteroids), or stony-iron (a mix of both).

Most of this debris, like the planets themselves, formed from — and still roughly orbits within — the same flattened plane as Earth's own orbit, the . That's why the asteroid belt, the Kuiper Belt and short-period comets are usually found close to it, while long-period comets from the roughly spherical Oort Cloud can arrive from any angle at all. See Retrograde Motion and Planetary Alignments for more on the ecliptic and the zodiacal band around it.

Transits of Venus and measuring the AU

First draft, to be checked by the teacher — comparing the two tracks measures Venus's shift against the Sun, which (with Venus's own orbital distance, already known from Kepler's third law) gives the astronomical unit.

A transit of Venus is Venus passing directly across the Sun's disc, as seen from Earth — rare, because Venus's orbit is tilted about 3.4° to Earth's. Transits come in pairs eight years apart, then nothing for over a century (roughly 105 or 121 years) before the next pair.

In the 18th century, the astronomer Edmond Halley proposed using a transit of Venus to measure the Sun-Earth distance (the AU) for the first time. The idea rests on : Venus, much closer to Earth than the Sun is, appears to shift slightly against the Sun's disc depending on where on Earth you're observing from. Two observers at widely separated latitudes see Venus trace very slightly different paths across the Sun, timed to start and end at slightly different moments. That tiny measured difference, combined with the precisely known distance between the two observers on Earth's surface, gives enough geometry to triangulate the real Sun-Earth distance.

Expeditions to observe the 1761 and 1769 transits (and later ones in 1874 and 1882) for exactly this reason are a famous episode in the history of astronomy — this page gives the principle only; the historical expeditions themselves aren't covered here yet.

Formation theories (brief)

Two open questions, covered only briefly here — both are covered in full on the planetary-formation page (Topic 12, not yet built on this site).

Why gas giants are gas giants

First draft, to be checked by the teacher — the frost line matters for core accretion, not disc instability; once formed, a giant planet's orbit isn't necessarily fixed where it started.

The leading theory, core accretion, has a solid core of ice and rock grow large enough to gravitationally pull in a massive envelope of hydrogen and helium gas straight from the disc of material the young Sun was still surrounded by. A less-favoured alternative, disc instability, has part of the disc itself become gravitationally unstable and collapse directly into a gas giant, skipping the slow core-building step.

First draft, to be checked by the teacher — both of the following are new and not yet verified against the spec or notes: beyond the frost line, ice, not just rock, can condense and add to a growing core — which is what core accretion needs, not disc instability, which instead needs the outer disc to be cold and massive enough to collapse on its own. And a gas giant isn't necessarily stuck where it formed either — giant planets are thought to migrate, drifting inward or outward through the disc after forming, which is one reason other planetary systems have been found with a "hot Jupiter": a giant planet in a very close, scorching orbit, far closer to its star than Jupiter is to the Sun.

Where Earth's water came from

Three candidate sources of Earth's water — first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Competing theories include water-rich asteroids colliding with the early Earth, icy comets doing the same, and volcanic outgassing as Earth itself cooled. None of these is settled as the sole explanation: scientists compare evidence such as the hydrogen isotope ratios in candidate sources against those measured in Earth's own oceans, to judge how much each may have contributed — still an active area of research.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: a dwarf planet (e.g. Pluto, Ceres) fails only the third IAU test — it orbits the Sun and is round, but hasn't cleared its orbit of other debris. It's still round and still orbits the Sun; what makes it "dwarf" is sharing its neighbourhood with other bodies. What makes a dwarf planet? ↑

Common exam confusion: meteoroid, meteor and meteorite are the same kind of object at three different stages, not three different kinds of object — meteoroid in space, meteor while burning up as a streak of light, meteorite if a fragment survives to reach the ground.
