Hubble's law, age and size

The further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away. Fit a line to find the Hubble constant, then use it to estimate the age and size of the Universe.

Further away, faster away

Distances from Cepheids; speeds from redshift (dashed line: the laboratory 656.3 nm line). The points lie on a straight line through the origin.

Two measurements together confirmed that the Universe is expanding. Distances to galaxies came from the Cepheid variable stars in them (see Variable stars), and their speeds from their redshift (see Redshift). Plotted on a graph, the points lie close to a straight line through the origin: the further away a galaxy is, the faster it is moving away from us. Speed in proportion to distance means the galaxies were once much closer together: the Universe is expanding.

Vesto Slipher measured the first galaxy redshifts in the 1910s; Edwin Hubble added Cepheid distances and published the graph in 1929. First draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Hubble's law: v = H0 × d

v = H0 × d

v is a galaxy's recession velocity (km/s), d its distance in (Mpc), and H0, the , is the gradient of the graph: (data sheet). To find a distance instead, divide both sides by H0: d = v ÷ H0.

    Uncertainty in H0

    Read H0 from your best-fit line: gradient = velocity ÷ distance, in km/s per Mpc. Then find the steepest and shallowest lines that still pass through every error bar: the uncertainty in H0 is half their range, (steepest − shallowest) ÷ 2. Now switch to the nearby galaxies only. The same-sized error bars let the lines swing much further, so the percentage uncertainty is bigger. Measuring over a wider range of distances (a longer measurement) makes the error bars a smaller fraction of the velocities, and so reduces the percentage uncertainty in H0.

    The points are made-up practice data. Each error bar stands for a galaxy's own motion through space, a few hundred km/s on top of the expansion: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

    The age and size of the Universe

    Run the expansion backwards. A galaxy at distance d, moving away at H0 × d, took d ÷ (H0 × d) = 1 ÷ H0 to get there, whatever d is. So every galaxy was together a time 1 ÷ H0 ago: an estimate of the age of the Universe. Converting the units, step by step:

      Nothing travels faster than light, so in that time light could have crossed about c × age. In light years that is the same number as the age in years: , a rough size for the part of the Universe we can see.

      Both are simple estimates: they assume the Universe has always expanded at today's rate, which is not quite true. The accepted age is about 13.8 billion years, and because space kept stretching while the light travelled, the most distant matter we can see is now about 46 billion light-years away.       Exam tips

      Common exam confusion: H0 is in km/s per Mpc. Change the Mpc into km first, so the km cancel and H0 is "per second"; only then work out 1 ÷ H0. The age and size of the Universe ↑

      Common exam confusion: a bigger H0 means faster expansion, so a younger Universe, not an older one. The age and size of the Universe ↑
