Infrared telescopes and high-altitude sites

Where a telescope can work depends on which waves get through the atmosphere, and how much the air spoils the view.

Which telescopes can sit at sea level

Each bar shows how far that kind of wave gets down through the atmosphere. Only the full bars reach the ground.

✓ Reaches the ground   ∼ Partly absorbed   ✗ Absorbed high in the atmosphere

The atmosphere lets visible light and radio waves through to the ground, but absorbs most other wavelengths. So only optical and radio telescopes work well at sea level (see Radio telescopes). Infrared telescopes need a high, dry site. Ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray telescopes must go into space, because those rays are absorbed high in the atmosphere, far above the highest mountains. Why infrared telescopes go up mountains

Water vapour in the air absorbs much of the infrared coming from space. Most water vapour is in the lowest few kilometres of the atmosphere, so a telescope high on a dry mountain is above most of it, and more infrared gets through.

A simplified model: the share of water vapour above you roughly halves for every 1.4 km you climb. The sites, their heights (Mauna Kea, Hawaii, about 4.2 km; the Chajnantor plateau, Chile, about 5.0 km) and the model's numbers: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

What infrared astronomy found

Infrared passes through dust that blocks visible light, and cool things glow in infrared even when they are too cool to glow in visible light.

  • Protostars: stars still forming, hidden inside clouds of dust, show up in infrared because it passes through the dust.
  • Dust and molecular clouds: cold clouds of dust and gas, where stars form, glow in infrared though they give out no visible light.
  • Hotspots on moons: infrared shows hot volcanic spots, such as those on Jupiter's moon Io.

These three discoveries, and Io as the example: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

How the atmosphere spoils images

Pockets of warmer and cooler air are always moving, and each bends starlight slightly differently. So a star's image dances about and spreads into a larger blur, which is why stars twinkle. The air also harms images in other ways:

  • Clouds block the light completely.
  • Scattering: air scatters light, so sunlight makes the day sky bright, and scattered street lighting (light pollution) brightens the night sky, hiding faint objects.
  • Absorption: the air absorbs some light, dimming stars, most of all near the horizon, where the light passes through more air.

A high site is also above much of the turbulent air, many clouds and most light pollution. These effects: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.
