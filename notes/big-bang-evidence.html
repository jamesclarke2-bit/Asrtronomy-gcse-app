Evidence for the Big Bang

Three observations show the Universe has changed over time, as the Big Bang theory predicts and the Steady State theory does not. Then: the tiny ripples that grew into galaxies.

Quasars: only far away

A schematic chart: the shape, not real counts.

Quasars are extremely luminous active galaxies (see Active galaxies). Every one found is far away, and there are none nearby. Light from far away left long ago, so quasars were common in the young Universe and are rare or gone today. The Universe has changed, as the Big Bang predicts; the Steady State theory says it always looks the same, so it cannot explain this.

That quasars are found only billions of light-years away, and the shape of this chart: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

The cosmic microwave background

The early Universe was hot and dense, and it glowed. As space expanded, that light was stretched to much longer wavelengths, into microwaves: the (CMB). It arrives from every direction, almost exactly the same, with the smooth spectrum of an object at about 2.7 K. The Big Bang predicts this cooled glow; the Steady State theory has no natural explanation for it.

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found the CMB by accident in 1965, as a hiss from every direction in a radio antenna; the 2.7 K temperature and the peak near 1 mm: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

The Hubble Deep Field

A drawing, not the real image.

The Hubble Space Telescope stared for days at a tiny patch of sky that looked empty. The image was full of faint, distant galaxies, seen as they were billions of years ago. Many are smaller and more irregular than galaxies nearby (see How galaxies form and change), so galaxies have changed over time, as the Big Bang predicts.

Taken in 1995 over about 10 days; the patch is about the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length, and holds about 3,000 galaxies: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Ripples in the CMB

The CMB is not perfectly even: it has tiny warmer and cooler patches. They mark places that were slightly denser in the early Universe. Gravity pulled more matter into them, growing them over billions of years into galaxies and clusters of galaxies: they are the seeds of everything we see (see How galaxies form and change). The WMAP and then the Planck spacecraft mapped them in finer and finer detail. Comparing the sizes and strengths of the patches with what each theory predicts tests ideas about how the Universe evolved: its age, what it is made of and how its structure grew.

The patches differ by about 1 part in 100,000; COBE made the first map (launched 1989), WMAP was launched in 2001 and Planck in 2009. First draft, to be checked by the teacher.
