Exoplanets and life

Planets round other stars are far too faint to see beside their star, so we find them by what they do to it. Then: where life could exist, how many civilisations there might be, and how we listen for them.

Finding planets 1: the transit method

If a planet's orbit is lined up edge-on to us, the planet passes in front of its star once every orbit and blocks a tiny fraction of its light: a transit. The star dims slightly, then recovers. The bigger the planet, the deeper the dip; the time between dips is the planet's orbital period. This is the same idea as an eclipsing binary, but with a planet instead of a second star.

The planets' sizes (an Earth-sized planet about 0.9%, a Neptune-sized one 3.5% and a Jupiter-sized one 10% of the Sun's width) and the 18-hour window: first draft, to be checked by the teacher. Small planets are drawn larger than true scale so they can be seen.

Finding planets 2: astrometry

A star and its planet both orbit their shared centre of mass. The star is far heavier, so its circle is tiny, but it does move. Astrometry means measuring a star's exact position on the sky very precisely, again and again, to spot that small wobble. A heavier planet, or one further out, makes a bigger wobble.

The wobble is drawn hugely exaggerated: for a star like the Sun with a Jupiter-like planet, it is far too small to see without very precise measurements. First draft, to be checked by the teacher.

Finding planets 3: radial velocity

As the star wobbles, it moves towards us for half an orbit and away for the other half. Its spectral lines shift towards blue, then towards red, then back, over and over: a Doppler shift (see Redshift). The time for one back-and-forth is the planet's orbital period; the size of the shift shows how hard the planet pulls, so how heavy it is. This is the radial velocity method.

The Goldilocks (habitable) zone

The habitable zone, or Goldilocks zone, is the range of distances from a star where a planet could be neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water on its surface. Liquid water is thought to be needed for life as we know it. A hotter star is brighter, so its zone is further out; a cool red dwarf's zone is very close in. Being in the zone does not mean a planet has life, only that liquid water is possible.

The zone's edges (about 0.95 to 1.67 times the square root of the star's luminosity, in AU; about 0.95 to 1.7 AU for the Sun) and the stars' brightness used here: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

The Drake equation

N = R* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L

N is the number of civilisations in our Galaxy sending signals we could detect. Each factor is explained beside its slider. Only the first two are measured with any confidence; the rest are guesses, so the answer ranges from far less than one (we are alone) to millions. The equation does not give an answer: it shows what we would need to know.

The choices on each slider, the guess sets, that only R* and fp are known with any confidence, the Drake equation's date (1961) and Frank Drake as its author: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.

SETI: listening by radio

SETI is the search for extraterrestrial intelligence. Most searches listen with radio telescopes for signals no natural source could make. Radio is used because it passes through dust in space and through the Earth's atmosphere, travels huge distances, takes little energy to send, and is what our own technology gives out. Any signal travels at the speed of light, so a reply from a star 100 light-years away would take at least 200 years to come back.

Project Ozma, the first radio search, by Frank Drake in 1960, and the Arecibo message sent in 1974: first draft, to be checked by the teacher.
