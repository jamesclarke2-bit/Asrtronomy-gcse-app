The Sun's structure

The Sun is layered, from a fusion-powered core outward to a wispy outer atmosphere visible only during a total eclipse or through specialised instruments.

Sunspots and the solar cycle

Sunspots are patches of the photosphere a little cooler than their surroundings — around 3,500–4,000°C, against the photosphere's usual 5,800 K (about 5,500°C) — caused by strong, tangled magnetic fields suppressing the convection that would otherwise carry heat up to the surface there — they look dark only by contrast with the brighter photosphere around them. The number of sunspots visible rises and falls over an 11-year solar cycle, from a quiet solar minimum to an active solar maximum and back again; sunspot number is the classic long-term index of solar activity.

The butterfly diagram

Plotting each sunspot's latitude against the date it was observed produces the "butterfly diagram": every cycle, sunspots first appear at high latitude (around 35°, in both hemispheres) and drift steadily toward the equator as the cycle progresses, before the next cycle begins again at high latitude. Drag the slider to see where a typical sunspot would sit at a given point in the pattern.

Two values are easy to read straight off the diagram: the gap between successive high-latitude starts gives the solar cycle length (about 11 years), and the latitude those starts happen at gives the latitude at cycle start (about 35°).

Worked example: estimating the Sun's rotation period

Because sunspots are carried around by the Sun's own rotation, tracking one from day to day across the visible disc gives a direct measurement of that rotation — the same idea as timing a rotating turntable by watching a mark on its edge.

A sunspot is measured at 20° east of the Sun's central meridian on day 1, and at 32° west of the central meridian on day 5.
Angular distance moved = 20° + 32° = 52°, over 4 days.
Rate = 52° ÷ 4 days = 13° per day.
Full rotation = 360° ÷ 13°/day ≈ 28 days.

This is the Sun's synodic rotation period — as seen from Earth, which is itself orbiting the Sun, the same distinction as the Moon's synodic month versus its sidereal one. The real observed synodic period is about 27.27 days; a single worked example like this rarely lands exactly on it, since it also depends on the latitude that particular sunspot happens to sit at — the Sun is a ball of plasma, not a solid body, and rotates faster at its equator (about 25 days, sidereal) than near its poles (up to about 35 days) — a behaviour called differential rotation.

The Maunder Minimum

Between 1645 and 1715, the Sun went through the Maunder Minimum — a prolonged dip in the solar cycle where sunspot numbers barely rose at all. At its deepest, from 1672 to 1699, fewer than 50 sunspots were recorded in total, against the 40,000–50,000 that would normally appear over a similar stretch. It coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age in Europe, and — independently of the historical sunspot counts — has since been confirmed by isotope data: proxies like carbon-14 and beryllium-10, produced when cosmic rays strike the atmosphere, spike during quiet solar periods (a weaker solar wind lets more cosmic rays through), and both show exactly this signature across 1645–1715.

Solar wind and its effects

The corona is so hot that some of its charged particles — electrons, protons and alpha particles — move fast enough to escape the Sun's gravity entirely, streaming outward through the Solar System at typical speeds of 300–800 km/s as the . It constantly influences every planet's magnetosphere; a few of its effects reach all the way to Earth and beyond:

  • Aurorae — Earth's magnetic field funnels incoming charged particles toward the poles, where they collide with atmospheric gases and make them glow, seen as the aurora borealis and aurora australis.
  • Cometary ion tails — the solar wind pushes a comet's ionised gas directly away from the Sun, so its ion tail always points away from the Sun regardless of which way the comet itself is travelling.
  • Geomagnetic storms — a surge in solar wind, often from a coronal mass ejection, temporarily disturbs Earth's magnetic field.
  • Satellites, aircraft and power grids — a strong geomagnetic storm can degrade GPS accuracy, disrupt satellite electronics and radio communications, raise radiation exposure on polar flight routes, and even induce damaging currents in power grids on the ground.

Van Allen belts

Earth's magnetic field doesn't just deflect the solar wind — it also permanently traps some of its charged particles in two doughnut-shaped regions called the : an inner belt (mostly energetic protons) and an outer belt (mostly electrons). Together they help shield the surface from this radiation — but pose a real risk to satellites and astronauts that spend time passing through or within them, whose electronics and health are both vulnerable to the trapped particles.

Exam tips

Common exam confusion: the Sun's rotation period (~25–35 days, how fast it spins) and the solar cycle (~11 years, how sunspot number rises and falls) are entirely different things, on wildly different timescales — don't mix them up just because both are described as "a period of the Sun".
