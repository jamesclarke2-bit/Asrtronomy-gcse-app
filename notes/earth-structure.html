Earth: Structure, Atmosphere and Motion

Earth isn't just the platform we observe from — its shape, its layered interior, its atmosphere and its motion through space all shape what astronomy is possible from its surface, and how.

Earth's shape and size

Earth is close to a perfect sphere, but not quite: it's an — very slightly flattened at the poles and slightly bulging at the equator, caused by its own rotation. The flattening is tiny compared to the whole planet: Earth's mean diameter is about 12,742 km, and the equatorial diameter is only around 43 km more than the polar diameter — a difference of roughly a third of one percent.

Inside the Earth: core, mantle and crust

Earth is layered, from a dense metallic core at the centre out to the thin rocky shell we live on.

Inner core Outer core Mantle Crust
  • Crust — thin, cool and brittle; the outer shell continents and oceans sit on.
  • Mantle — by far the thickest layer; semi-solid rock that flows extremely slowly over long timescales.
  • Outer core — molten iron-nickel; its churning motion generates Earth's magnetic field.
  • Inner core — solid iron-nickel, despite being the hottest layer, kept solid by immense pressure.

The atmosphere: composition and layers

By volume, dry air is overwhelmingly nitrogen and oxygen, with everything else a small remainder:

Water vapour is left out of the "dry air" percentages above because it varies so much — from almost none over a cold desert to a few percent in humid tropical air.

The atmosphere doesn't end sharply; it just keeps thinning until it's indistinguishable from the near-vacuum of space, by around 10,000 km up. Almost all of its mass, though, and everything that matters for weather and for observing, sits in a thin set of layers close to the surface:

Troposphere

The lowest layer. All of Earth's weather happens here, and it's as high as ordinary aircraft fly.

Stratosphere

Contains the ozone layer, which absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the Sun.

Mesosphere

Most meteors burn up here, heated by friction with the thin remaining air — this is what produces a "shooting star".

Thermosphere

Aurorae occur here, and it's where the International Space Station orbits.

Why the atmosphere matters for astronomy

The same atmosphere that makes Earth habitable both helps and hinders astronomy — often for the same underlying reason.

It helps

It absorbs most harmful UV, X-ray and gamma radiation before it reaches the ground (ozone molecules in the stratosphere are broken apart by UV, absorbing it in the process), regulates surface temperature by absorbing infrared radiation via molecular resonance and re-emitting it — the — supplies the oxygen life depends on, and burns up most small meteoroids before they reach the surface.

It hinders

Sunlight scattered across the whole sky means ground-based astronomy is largely restricted to night-time, and constant small variations in air density and temperature bend starlight unpredictably as it arrives, causing — the visible "twinkling" of stars, which blurs and distorts fine detail in any ground-based image or measurement.

Earth's motions

Earth moves in two distinct ways at once, plus a fixed tilt that doesn't change either motion but has a huge effect on what we see:

  • Rotation — Earth spins on its axis once every 24 hours, which is why we get day and night, and why the whole sky appears to wheel overhead through the night.
  • Revolution — Earth orbits the Sun once every 365.25 days, which is why the night sky's visible constellations slowly change through the year.
  • Axial tilt — Earth's axis is tilted 23.5° from vertical (relative to its orbit), and keeps pointing the same direction in space all year round. That fixed tilt, not any change in distance from the Sun, is what causes the seasons.
