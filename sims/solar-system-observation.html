Retrograde Motion and Planetary Alignments

Mercury, Venus and Mars each trace their own circle around the Sun, but Earth is doing the same, and that relative motion is what actually decides what we see: Mars briefly looping backwards against the stars, Venus never straying far from the Sun, Mercury all but lost in its glare. 1. Inner and outer planets

Mercury and Venus orbit closer to the Sun than Earth does — the . Mars and everything farther out orbit beyond Earth — the . An inferior planet's whole orbit sits inside Earth's, so it can never be on the far side of the Sun from Earth the way opposition needs — it only ever appears close to the Sun in our sky, swinging between an evening and a morning view either side of it.

2. Alignments

Every stage of the cycle has a name. Click one to jump the slider to the next time it happens.

Conjunction: planet and Sun in the same direction from Earth — inferior (the planet passes between Earth and the Sun) or superior (behind the Sun). Opposition: planet opposite the Sun — closest, brightest, rises as the Sun sets.

Transits — the planet crossing the Sun's own disc — can only happen at inferior conjunction, and even then they're rare: Mercury's and Venus's orbits are tilted slightly relative to Earth's, so most inferior conjunctions pass above or below the Sun rather than across it.

Transit and occultation

Two more precise alignments, rare enough that they're better understood from a diagram than watched on a slider.

Venus, crossing the Sun's disc as seen from Earth

Transit

A transit is when a nearer body crosses directly in front of a farther one, as seen from Earth, appearing as a small dark dot moving across its disc. Only Mercury and Venus — the two planets closer to the Sun than Earth — can transit the Sun, and only when they pass between Earth and the Sun at a point where their (slightly tilted) orbit also crosses Earth's line of sight, which is why transits are rare: Venus transits come in pairs eight years apart, separated by over a century.

The Moon, having just occulted (hidden) a star, as seen from Earth

Occultation

An occultation is when a nearer body passes directly in front of a farther one and completely hides it, as seen from Earth — unlike a transit, the farther body disappears rather than remaining visible as a dot. The Moon, being both nearby and relatively large in the sky, most often occults stars and planets that happen to lie along its path; a solar eclipse is really just the Moon occulting the Sun.
