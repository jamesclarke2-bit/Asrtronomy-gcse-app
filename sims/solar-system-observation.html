Retrograde Motion and Planetary Alignments

Mars orbits the Sun more slowly than Earth does — so roughly every 26 months, Earth "overtakes" it on the inside track, and Mars briefly appears to move backwards against the background stars. Watch it happen below, and see how conjunction, opposition and elongation are just different stages of the same repeating cycle.

Transit and occultation

Two more precise alignments, rare enough that they're better understood from a diagram than watched on a slider.

Venus, crossing the Sun's disc as seen from Earth

Transit

A transit is when a nearer body crosses directly in front of a farther one, as seen from Earth, appearing as a small dark dot moving across its disc. Only Mercury and Venus — the two planets closer to the Sun than Earth — can transit the Sun, and only when they pass between Earth and the Sun at a point where their (slightly tilted) orbit also crosses Earth's line of sight, which is why transits are rare: Venus transits come in pairs eight years apart, separated by over a century.

The Moon, having just occulted (hidden) a star, as seen from Earth

Occultation

An occultation is when a nearer body passes directly in front of a farther one and completely hides it, as seen from Earth — unlike a transit, the farther body disappears rather than remaining visible as a dot. The Moon, being both nearby and relatively large in the sky, most often occults stars and planets that happen to lie along its path; a solar eclipse is really just the Moon occulting the Sun.
