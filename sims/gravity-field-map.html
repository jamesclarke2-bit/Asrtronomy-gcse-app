Gravitational Field Maps and Lagrange Points

Drag two masses around and watch their combined field, its equipotential contours, and the point where their pulls cancel, redraw live — then switch to the frame rotating with them, where that same two-body system turns out to have five points of balance altogether, not one.

The zero-field point is not L1

For GCSE: at the zero-field point between Earth and the Moon, the two pulls exactly cancel, so a mass there feels no net force at that instant — but it would not circle Earth in step with the Moon, and would simply drift away from the Earth-Moon line.

For A-level: at L1, the Moon's pull only partly cancels Earth's, so the net inward pull is smaller than Earth's alone — exactly small enough that it supplies the centripetal force for an orbit with the Moon's own period, one lunar month. That net pull being smaller (not zero) is what lets a body at L1 stay in line with the Moon, which is exactly why L1 sits a little closer to Earth than the point of full cancellation.

The map below marks both: a zero-field point (plain superposition of the two fields, no rotation involved) and, when the effective-potential view is switched on, L1 itself (found from the rotating frame). They're close, but never the same point unless the two masses are equal.

1. The map

Drag either mass anywhere on the map. Gravitational field draws the combined field's direction (arrows), a few equipotential contours, and the zero-field point. Effective potential switches to the frame rotating with the two masses, shading it from deep wells (dark) to the L4/L5 hills (light), and marks all five Lagrange points — L1, L2 and L3 sit in saddles along the line through both masses; L4 and L5 sit at the top of hills, forming an equilateral triangle with them.

2. Mass-ratio explorer: when are L4 and L5 stable?

L4 and L5 sit at the same geometric spot — an equilateral triangle with the two masses — no matter what those two masses actually are. Whether a body sitting there stays nearby is a different question, and it depends entirely on the mass ratio. Drag the slider and watch the label flip.

Stability threshold (Routh's criterion)
L4 / L5

Marked on the slider: the real Earth-Moon mass ratio (about 81, comfortably stable) and the threshold itself (about 25, where the label flips). The real Sun-Earth ratio is about 333,000 — so far beyond this slider's range that it can't be marked on it at all.

Why doesn't it just slide off the hill? The effective potential really is a hill at a stable L4 or L5 — gravity and the rotating frame's own outward push would slide a displaced mass away on their own. What saves it is the Coriolis force, which only acts once something is moving in the rotating frame and always curves that motion sideways rather than letting it coast downhill; above the mass-ratio threshold, that curving is enough to turn a slide into a stable loop around the point instead.
