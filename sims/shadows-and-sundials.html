Shadows and Sundials

A shadow stick finds true north and local noon from its own shortest shadow, a sundial reads the time directly from that same shadow, and the Equation of Time turns a noon reading into your own longitude.

1. Shadow stick: finding true north and local noon

Shadow tip across the day   Current shadow   Shortest shadow (local noon)

Reading true north from a shadow

The shadow is shortest exactly when the Sun is highest for the day — local apparent noon. North of the Sun's declination the shadow then points due north; south of it, due south (the dashed curve above traces every shadow tip through the day, and the ringed marker shows that shortest point). The "Find north and noon with equal shadows" steps below find the same north-south line without timing the exact minimum: move the time slider to any moment before noon, and its mirror-image moment after noon casts an equally long shadow, since shadow length depends only on how far from noon you are, not which side — the perpendicular bisector of the line joining those two tips passes exactly through true north-south.

Worked example: a shadow marked at 09:12 in the morning and one of the same length at 14:48 in the afternoon have a midpoint of (09:12 + 14:48) ÷ 2 = 12:00 — so that day's local apparent noon is 12:00, and the line joining the two marks runs due east-west.

Find your longitude from a shadow and a clock

What this is for: finding your longitude from nothing but a shadow and a clock — no map, compass or GPS. The equation of time shown above only depends on the date, not on where you are — it's the same quantity the Equation of Time page calculates.

Find the time (UT) of the shortest shadow, using the UT time slider.

Drag the slider back and forth and watch "Shadow length now" above it: the shortest reading is local apparent noon at the hidden longitude.

Look up the equation of time for this date (shown above), and work out the UT at which the Sun would cross the meridian at longitude 0°:

UT of longitude-0° noon = 12:00 − equation of time

A positive equation of time means the Sun is running ahead of clock time — subtract it from 12:00. Subtracting a negative value adds instead.

The difference between your time (step 1) and the longitude-0° time (step 2) is 4 minutes per degree of longitude — and east is earlier, since the Sun reaches an eastern longitude's meridian before it reaches Greenwich's.

longitude = (longitude-0° time − your time) ÷ 4 minutes/degree

Your noon earlier than the longitude-0° time means east (positive); later means west (negative).

Enter your longitude:

longitude = 15 × (12:00 − equation of time − UT of local noon) — the same relationship, rearranged into one line.

Worked example. Step 1: the shortest shadow is found at 11:42 UT. Step 2: that day's equation of time is +3.4 min, so the longitude-0° noon is 12:00 − 3.4 min = 11:56.6 UT. Step 3: the difference is 11:42 − 11:56.6 = −14.6 min — your noon came earlier, so you're east. Step 4: 14.6 min ÷ 4 min/degree = 3.65°, so the longitude is 3.65° E.

3. Sundial

Why the hour lines aren't evenly spaced

The gnomon — the dial's raised, triangular edge — is tilted to the observer's own latitude and points at the celestial pole, so its shadow sweeps round at the Sun's own steady 15°/hour rate. Projected onto the flat dial plate, though, that steady sweep bunches up near noon and spreads out near sunrise and sunset:

tan(hour-line angle) = sin(latitude) × tan(15° × hours from noon)

Only at latitude 90° (a pole) does sin(latitude) = 1 leave the hour lines evenly spaced, exactly 15° apart. A sundial reads apparent solar time directly — exactly what a shadow stick measures — which is not what a clock shows. See the Equation of Time page for one of the four reasons why; toggle the readout below for all four together.

In a UK summer, clocks are put forward an hour for British Summer Time, so a sundial then reads about an hour behind the clock, on top of the other three effects above.
