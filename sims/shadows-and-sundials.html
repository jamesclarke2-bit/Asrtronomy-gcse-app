Shadows and Sundials

A shadow stick finds true north and local noon from its own shortest shadow, a sundial reads the time directly from that same shadow, and the Equation of Time turns a noon reading into your own longitude.

1. Shadow stick: finding true north and local noon

Shadow tip across the day   Current shadow   Shortest shadow (local noon)

Reading true north from a shadow

The shadow is shortest exactly when the Sun is highest for the day — local apparent noon. North of the Sun's declination the shadow then points due north; south of it, due south (the dashed curve above traces every shadow tip through the day, and the ringed marker shows that shortest point). Tick "Show equal-shadow-length construction" to find the same north-south line without timing the exact minimum: move the time slider to any moment before noon, and its mirror-image moment after noon casts an equally long shadow, since shadow length depends only on how far from noon you are, not which side — the perpendicular bisector of the line joining those two tips passes exactly through true north-south.

2. Challenge: find your longitude

From a noon time to a longitude

The equation of time shown above only depends on the date, not on where you are — it's the same quantity the Equation of Time page calculates. Scrub the challenge time slider until the shadow is as short as it gets: that moment's UT clock time is the UT of local apparent noon.

UT of local noon = 12:00 − equation of time − longitude ÷ 15 hours

Rearranged for longitude (east positive, so a negative answer is west):

longitude = 15 × (12:00 − equation of time − UT of local noon)

3. Sundial

Hour lines   Gnomon's shadow now

Why the hour lines aren't evenly spaced

The gnomon — the dial's raised, triangular edge — is tilted to the observer's own latitude and points at the celestial pole, so its shadow sweeps round at the Sun's own steady 15°/hour rate. Projected onto the flat dial plate, though, that steady sweep bunches up near noon and spreads out near sunrise and sunset:

tan(hour-line angle) = sin(latitude) × tan(15° × hours from noon)

Only at latitude 90° (a pole) does sin(latitude) = 1 leave the hour lines evenly spaced, exactly 15° apart. A sundial reads apparent solar time directly — exactly what a shadow stick measures — which is not what a clock shows. See the Equation of Time page for one of the three reasons why; toggle the readout below for all three together.

How sundial time and clock time differ

Comparing a homemade sundial's reading against a known clock time, corrected this way, is also a recognised way to check a sundial's own accuracy — observation task A10 on the specification.
