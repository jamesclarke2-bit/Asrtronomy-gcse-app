Top-down: Earth, Moon and sunlight

Earth   Sunlight direction (fixed)

The Moon is always half-lit on the side facing the Sun — that never changes. What changes as it orbits is how much of that lit half we can see from Earth.

The orbit is an ellipse with Earth off-centre, so the Moon's distance changes as it goes round. Its oval shape is exaggerated 3× here so you can see it — the real orbit is much closer to a circle.

Common exam confusion: phases are caused by the Moon's changing position relative to the Sun — and so how much of its permanently half-lit surface faces Earth — not by Earth's shadow falling on the Moon. Earth's shadow on the Moon is a completely separate, much rarer event: a , which only happens when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up almost exactly — unlike an ordinary phase, which happens every single day of every month.

Perigee, apogee and supermoons

The Moon's orbit isn't a perfect circle but a slightly stretched ellipse, with Earth off-centre at one focus. Its mean distance is about 384,400 km, but it swings in to about 363,300 km at its closest point, , and out to about 405,500 km at its farthest, . It also moves fastest near perigee and slowest near apogee, so its distance on any given day comes from its actual angle from perigee, not just the steady angle that sets its phase.

Perigee comes round every ~27.55 days, but the phases take ~29.53, so the two drift in and out of step. When a full or new Moon happens to fall within a few days of perigee, it's popularly called a . A full Moon at perigee can look up to about 14% larger and about 30% brighter than one at apogee. A new-Moon supermoon can't be seen, but its extra pull shows up as unusually large spring tides.
