Observing the Sun

The Sun's declination shifts with the date as Earth's tilted axis turns — and that changes its altitude at transit (solar noon) for any given latitude. The classic exam question: "what's the Sun's altitude at transit at latitude X on date Y?"

This page covers how the Sun's position in the sky changes through the year, and how to observe it safely.

    Observing the Sun safely

    Never look directly at the Sun — not with the naked eye, and never through binoculars, a telescope or a camera viewfinder without a proper solar filter. Its light and heat can burn the retina permanently within seconds. The retina has no pain receptors, so this damage happens without any warning and often without you noticing until afterwards.

    The safest way to view the Sun's disc directly is through solar filters (or "eclipse glasses") specifically certified to the ISO 12312-2 standard, which fit over the front of your eyes, binoculars or telescope. Ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass and exposed camera film are all not safe — they don't block the invisible infrared and ultraviolet light that does the damage.

    A safer option that needs no special equipment at all is projection:

    1. Pierce a small, clean hole in the centre of a piece of card.
    2. With your back to the Sun, hold the card up so sunlight passes through the hole.
    3. Hold a second, plain card in the hole's shadow, a short distance behind it, as a screen.
    4. An inverted image of the Sun's disc appears on the screen card — look at that image, never at the Sun or through the hole itself.

    Card with a pinhole (left), casting an inverted image of the Sun onto a screen card (right)

    Telescope projection

    For an image large and sharp enough to see sunspots directly, project through a small telescope instead of a simple pinhole:

    1. Use a small refractor (about 60–80mm aperture) with a low-power eyepiece (25–40×).
    2. Cap or cover the finder scope, so nobody looks through it by mistake.
    3. Hold or clamp a plain card 20–50cm behind the eyepiece.
    4. Focus the telescope until the Sun's edge (its limb) is sharp on the card.

    Safety: never look through the eyepiece or finder scope — only ever look at the projected image on the card. Never leave the telescope unattended while it's pointed at the Sun: concentrated sunlight is a fire risk, and anyone else nearby could look through it without knowing better. Keep sessions brief, since heat builds up inside the eyepiece and tube. Never use a plastic eyepiece for this — the concentrated heat can melt it.

    This method is good for observing sunspots directly, and — using the same tracking idea as the worked example below — for estimating the Sun's rotation period.

    A small refractor's eyepiece (centre) projects a magnified, focused image of the Sun — including sunspots — onto a card held behind it

    H-alpha filters

    H-alpha solar filters must be dedicated, front-mounted systems, fitted over the full aperture at the front of the telescope — never an eyepiece-end filter (these sit at the telescope's concentrated focal point and can crack or fail from the heat) and never a photographic or imaging-only H-alpha filter used for direct visual observation (these aren't built to safely handle the full, concentrated energy your eye would receive).

    A correctly mounted H-alpha filter isolates an extremely narrow band of light at 656.28 nm (deep red) — the hydrogen-alpha spectral line — blocking almost everything else. This reveals features completely invisible in ordinary white light: prominences (loops of glowing gas arcing above the edge of the disc), filaments (the same kind of feature seen from above, as dark threads crossing the disc), plage (bright patches near active regions), and spicules (short-lived jets of gas across the surface).

    X-ray imaging

    The Sun also emits X-rays, but Earth's atmosphere absorbs them before they reach the ground — so X-ray telescopes must be based in space, in orbit above the atmosphere, to see them at all.

    On an X-ray image of the Sun, the bright white patches are active regions: sites of intense, tangled magnetic activity, often around sunspot groups — the same regions responsible for solar flares.

    Sunspots and the solar cycle

    Sunspots are patches of the photosphere a little cooler than their surroundings (around 3,500–4,000°C, against the photosphere's usual 5,500°C), caused by strong, tangled magnetic fields suppressing the convection that would otherwise carry heat up to the surface there — they look dark only by contrast with the brighter photosphere around them. The number of sunspots visible rises and falls over an 11-year solar cycle, from a quiet solar minimum to an active solar maximum and back again; sunspot number is the classic long-term index of solar activity.

    Worked example: estimating the Sun's rotation period

    Because sunspots are carried around by the Sun's own rotation, tracking one from day to day across the visible disc gives a direct measurement of that rotation — the same idea as timing a rotating turntable by watching a mark on its edge.

    A sunspot is measured at 20° east of the Sun's central meridian on day 1, and at 32° west of the central meridian on day 5.
    Angular distance moved = 20° + 32° = 52°, over 4 days.
    Rate = 52° ÷ 4 days = 13° per day.
    Full rotation = 360° ÷ 13°/day ≈ 28 days.

    This is the Sun's synodic rotation period — as seen from Earth, which is itself orbiting the Sun, the same distinction as the Moon's synodic month versus its sidereal one. The real observed synodic period is about 27.27 days; a single worked example like this rarely lands exactly on it, since it also depends on the latitude that particular sunspot happens to sit at — the Sun is a ball of plasma, not a solid body, and rotates faster at its equator (about 25 days, sidereal) than near its poles (up to about 35 days) — a behaviour called differential rotation.

    Common exam confusion: the Sun's rotation period (~25–35 days, how fast it spins) and the solar cycle (~11 years, how sunspot number rises and falls) are entirely different things, on wildly different timescales — don't mix them up just because both are described as "a period of the Sun".

    The butterfly diagram

    Plotting each sunspot's latitude against the date it was observed produces the "butterfly diagram": every cycle, sunspots first appear at high latitude (around 35°, in both hemispheres) and drift steadily toward the equator as the cycle progresses, before the next cycle begins again at high latitude. Drag the slider to see where a typical sunspot would sit at a given point in the pattern.

    Two values are easy to read straight off the diagram: the gap between successive high-latitude starts gives the solar cycle length (about 11 years), and the latitude those starts happen at gives the latitude at cycle start (about 35°).

    The Maunder Minimum

    Between 1645 and 1715, the Sun went through the Maunder Minimum — a prolonged dip in the solar cycle where sunspot numbers barely rose at all. At its deepest, from 1672 to 1699, fewer than 50 sunspots were recorded in total, against the 40,000–50,000 that would normally appear over a similar stretch. It coincided with the coldest part of the Little Ice Age in Europe, and — independently of the historical sunspot counts — has since been confirmed by isotope data: proxies like carbon-14 and beryllium-10, produced when cosmic rays strike the atmosphere, spike during quiet solar periods (a weaker solar wind lets more cosmic rays through), and both show exactly this signature across 1645–1715.

    Solar wind and its effects

    The corona is so hot that some of its charged particles — electrons, protons and alpha particles — move fast enough to escape the Sun's gravity entirely, streaming outward through the Solar System at typical speeds of 300–800 km/s as the . It constantly influences every planet's magnetosphere; a few of its effects reach all the way to Earth and beyond:

    • Aurorae — Earth's magnetic field funnels incoming charged particles toward the poles, where they collide with atmospheric gases and make them glow, seen as the aurora borealis and aurora australis.
    • Cometary ion tails — the solar wind pushes a comet's ionised gas directly away from the Sun, so its ion tail always points away from the Sun regardless of which way the comet itself is travelling.
    • Geomagnetic storms — a surge in solar wind, often from a coronal mass ejection, temporarily disturbs Earth's magnetic field.
    • Satellites, aircraft and power grids — a strong geomagnetic storm can degrade GPS accuracy, disrupt satellite electronics and radio communications, raise radiation exposure on polar flight routes, and even induce damaging currents in power grids on the ground.

    Van Allen belts

    Earth's magnetic field doesn't just deflect the solar wind — it also permanently traps some of its charged particles in two doughnut-shaped regions called the : an inner belt (mostly energetic protons) and an outer belt (mostly electrons). Together they help shield the surface from this radiation — but pose a real risk to satellites and astronauts that spend time passing through or within them, whose electronics and health are both vulnerable to the trapped particles.
