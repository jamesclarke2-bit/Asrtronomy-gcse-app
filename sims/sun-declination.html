Observing the Sun

The Sun's declination shifts with the date as Earth's tilted axis turns — and that changes its altitude at transit (solar noon) for any given latitude. The classic exam question: "what's the Sun's altitude at transit at latitude X on date Y?"

This page covers how the Sun's position in the sky changes through the year, and how to observe it safely.

    Observing the Sun safely

    Never look directly at the Sun — not with the naked eye, and never through binoculars, a telescope or a camera viewfinder without a proper solar filter. Its light and heat can burn the retina permanently within seconds. The retina has no pain receptors, so this damage happens without any warning and often without you noticing until afterwards.

    The safest way to view the Sun's disc directly is through solar filters (or "eclipse glasses") specifically certified to the ISO 12312-2 standard, which fit over the front of your eyes, binoculars or telescope. Ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass and exposed camera film are all not safe — they don't block the invisible infrared and ultraviolet light that does the damage.

    A safer option that needs no special equipment at all is projection:

    Card with a pinhole (left), casting an inverted image of the Sun onto a screen card (right)

    1. Pierce a small, clean hole in the centre of a piece of card.
    2. With your back to the Sun, hold the card up so sunlight passes through the hole.
    3. Hold a second, plain card in the hole's shadow, a short distance behind it, as a screen.
    4. An inverted image of the Sun's disc appears on the screen card — look at that image, never at the Sun or through the hole itself.

    Telescope projection

    For an image large and sharp enough to see sunspots directly, project through a small telescope instead of a simple pinhole:

    A small refractor's eyepiece (centre) projects a magnified, focused image of the Sun — including sunspots — onto a card held behind it

    1. Use a small refractor (about 60–80mm aperture) with a low-power eyepiece (25–40×).
    2. Cap or cover the finder scope, so nobody looks through it by mistake.
    3. Hold or clamp a plain card 20–50cm behind the eyepiece.
    4. Focus the telescope until the Sun's edge (its limb) is sharp on the card.

    Safety: never look through the eyepiece or finder scope — only ever look at the projected image on the card. Never leave the telescope unattended while it's pointed at the Sun: concentrated sunlight is a fire risk, and anyone else nearby could look through it without knowing better. Keep sessions brief, since heat builds up inside the eyepiece and tube. Never use a plastic eyepiece for this — the concentrated heat can melt it.

    This method is good for observing sunspots directly, and — using the same tracking idea as the worked example below — for estimating the Sun's rotation period.

    H-alpha filters

    H-alpha solar filters must be dedicated, front-mounted systems, fitted over the full aperture at the front of the telescope — never an eyepiece-end filter (these sit at the telescope's concentrated focal point and can crack or fail from the heat) and never a photographic or imaging-only H-alpha filter used for direct visual observation (these aren't built to safely handle the full, concentrated energy your eye would receive).

    A correctly mounted H-alpha filter isolates an extremely narrow band of light at 656.28 nm (deep red) — the hydrogen-alpha spectral line — blocking almost everything else. This reveals features completely invisible in ordinary white light: prominences (loops of glowing gas arcing above the edge of the disc), filaments (the same kind of feature seen from above, as dark threads crossing the disc), plage (bright patches near active regions), and spicules (short-lived jets of gas across the surface).

    X-ray imaging

    The Sun also emits X-rays, but Earth's atmosphere absorbs them before they reach the ground — so X-ray telescopes must be based in space, in orbit above the atmosphere, to see them at all.

    On an X-ray image of the Sun, the bright white patches are active regions: sites of intense, tangled magnetic activity, often around sunspot groups — the same regions responsible for solar flares.
