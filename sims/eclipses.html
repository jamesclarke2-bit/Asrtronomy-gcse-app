Eclipses

There's a new Moon and a full Moon every month, so why isn't there an eclipse every month? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted — pick a date and see where the Moon sits relative to the line where its orbit crosses Earth's.

Solar and lunar eclipses

Solar eclipse

Happens at new Moon, when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and Earth — visible only from the narrow path where the umbra touches the ground.

Lunar eclipse

Happens at full Moon, when the Moon passes into Earth's shadow — visible from the whole night side of Earth at once.

Why there isn't an eclipse every month

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5.1° to the (the plane of Earth's orbit round the Sun). It crosses that plane at just two points, the ascending and descending nodes, joined by the . At most new and full Moons, the Moon is well away from a node, so it passes a little above or below the Sun (no solar eclipse) or above or below Earth's shadow (no lunar eclipse).

An eclipse is only possible when a new or full Moon happens close to a node — within the :

Ecliptic limits (maximum distance from a node)
EclipseAt least partialTotal
Solar (at new Moon)18.4°11.8°
Lunar (at full Moon)12.2°5.9°

The Sun sweeps past each node roughly every 173 days, so eclipses cluster into eclipse seasons about six months apart — use the Next/Previous buttons and watch the line of nodes swing round to point at the Sun.

"Possible" doesn't mean "certain": these are the widest limits, reached only when the Sun and Moon are at their closest. Near the edge, what actually happens depends on their distances — which is what the shadow diagram works out. On 25 March 2024, for example, the full Moon was about 11° from a node, inside the lunar limit, but it only passed through the faint outer penumbra.

What kind of eclipse?

The Sun is a big light source, so every shadow in space has two parts: a dark central , where the Sun is completely blocked, and a fainter outer , where it's only partly blocked.

  • Solar eclipses — the Moon's umbra is a cone about 374,000 km long, but the Moon is 356,000–407,000 km away. When the Moon is near perigee, the cone's tip reaches Earth: a total eclipse, seen from a narrow path. Near apogee it falls short, the Moon looks slightly smaller than the Sun, and a bright ring is left: an annular eclipse. If the umbra's axis misses Earth altogether, only the penumbra touches it: a partial eclipse.
  • Lunar eclipses — Earth's shadow is far bigger than the Moon. If the Moon passes wholly into the umbra it's total (and often turns coppery red, lit only by sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere); partly into it, partial; and if it only crosses the penumbra, a barely noticeable penumbral eclipse.

This page takes the Moon's distance from its simplified elliptical orbit, whose shape is fixed (perigee always 363,300 km). Real perigees vary, so real shadow widths can differ — on 8 April 2024 the real Moon was about 6,000 km closer than this model puts it, making the real path of totality (up to ~198 km) wider than the ~135 km shown here. Which type of eclipse it was comes out the same.
