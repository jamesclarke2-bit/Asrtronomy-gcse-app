Orbits and Gravity

Newton's own argument for why the Moon doesn't fall out of the sky: fire a cannonball fast enough sideways, and "falling" and "orbiting" become the same thing. Watch a real, step-by-step simulation of gravity alone turn a launch speed into a path, then see why gravity's strength depends on distance and mass the way it does.

1. Newton's cannon

Every shaded wedge covers an equal time interval, and every wedge has the same area — exactly like the wedges on Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion, but this time nothing about ellipses or equal areas was assumed in advance. The path above comes purely from numerically stepping gravity (F = GMm/r²) forward in tiny increments; the equal areas, and the ellipse itself, fall straight out of that — exactly what Newton showed.

Fired from a schematic 10 km tower, like Newton's own very-tall-mountain thought experiment, so the cannonball has real ground to fall to below its launch point. Not to scale: that 10 km gap is stretched out visually so it's actually visible next to Earth's 6,371 km radius — the simulation itself uses the true, un-exaggerated altitude throughout, only the drawing is stretched.

Air resistance is ignored completely. A real object launched from only 10 km up would still be deep in the atmosphere and would burn up or slow dramatically long before tracing any of these paths — this is a vacuum, gravity-only idealisation, the same one Newton's own thought experiment imagined by firing from above the atmosphere entirely.

2. Inverse-square law

Newton's cannon presets

Dragging close to circular speed locks on to it exactly, rather than landing on the nearest slider step. That's deliberate, not a glitch: orbits this close to the ground are extremely sensitive, and a speed only a fraction of a percent too slow sends the path straight into the ground instead of around it — the snap is what keeps a deliberate circular launch from becoming an accidental crash.

Marked on the slider: circular orbit speed () and escape speed (). Escape speed here is the same escape velocity a rocket needs to reach the Moon — see Escape velocity and rockets on The Moon: Structure, Surface and Origin rather than re-deriving it here.
