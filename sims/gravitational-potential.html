Gravitational Potential Energy and Orbital Energy

Why is gravitational potential energy always negative? Why does mgh stop working once you're far from a surface? And what actually happens to a satellite's energy as its orbit changes? All three come from the same engine — a point mass's gravitational field, g(r) = GM/r² — worked through honestly rather than quoted.

Defining gravitational potential energy

Start from the external agent, not the field, because that's the definition: gravitational potential energy is the work an external agent does bringing a mass in from infinity to some point, moving it at constant speed the whole way. "Constant speed" matters — it means kinetic energy never changes during the move, so every bit of work the agent does goes straight into potential energy, with nothing left over to argue about.

Equivalently, since the field is conservative, that work is exactly minus the work gravity itself does over the same move:

ΔU = Wagent = −Wfield

Why the opposite sign? Gravity pulls the mass inward on its own, every step of the way in from infinity. To keep the speed constant against that pull, the agent has to push back — outward, opposite to the direction the mass is actually moving — and a force applied opposite to motion is, by definition, negative work. So the agent's work (potential energy) comes out negative precisely because gravity's own work, over that same inward move, is positive. Flip the move around — haul the mass back out to infinity — and every sign in that paragraph flips too.

That's also why V(r) = −GM/r is negative everywhere and zero only at infinity: zero is simply where the agent starts, before having done any work at all, and every step closer costs (in the agent's bookkeeping) a bit more negative work.

Released from rest, nothing but gravity acts on it: the field's work all becomes kinetic energy, and potential energy falls by exactly that much — so the total, kinetic plus potential, never changes. It stays at the value it started with, for as long as the mass is actually in free fall.

This is the definition in practice: potential energy is defined as the work an external agent does bringing a mass in at constant speed, which is exactly why the "work done by the hand" row above and the "potential energy change" row above it show the same number — they're not two separate facts that happen to agree, they're the same fact stated twice.

Lowering all the way from the release point to the surface, the hand's total work approaches −GM/R as the release point moves out — and gets there faster than you might expect: 90% of the way there at 10 radii out, 99% at 100 radii. "Infinity" is not far away in practice.

2. The potential well, V(r) = −GM/r

Plotting V(r) against r draws a "well": deepest (most negative) at the surface, rising — getting less negative — as r grows, flattening out as it approaches zero. Zero itself is a convention, not a special physical place: it's wherever the external agent of section 1 would have started, which by definition is infinity. "Higher" on this graph always means less negative, never positive — and escaping means climbing all the way out, up to that zero line.

3. mgh vs the exact formula

mgh assumes g stays constant over the climb — true only close to a surface. The exact potential energy gained climbing from radius R to R + h is

ΔU = GMm(1/R − 1/(R+h))

Slide the height up from a few metres to thousands of kilometres and watch the exact value and mgh pull apart — and the ratio between them drift away from 1 as h stops being small compared with R.

5. A satellite's energy budget

A circular orbit has kinetic energy (½v², from v = √(GM/r)), potential energy (−GM/r) and their sum, the total. Raise the orbit radius and watch kinetic energy fall — a higher orbit is a slower one — while potential energy rises faster than kinetic energy falls, so the total rises too (becomes less negative), even though the satellite is moving slower, not faster.

6. Escaping: total energy reaching zero

Total specific energy (kinetic + potential) is conserved, so its sign at launch decides the outcome everywhere along the path: negative and it can never reach infinity (bound — it falls back or settles into an orbit); exactly zero and it just barely reaches infinity, arriving with nothing left (this is exactly where escape speed comes from, and the same thing escape velocity on The Moon: Structure, Surface and Origin derives from); positive and it's unbound with speed to spare.

classifyOrbit below is tolerance-aware: a speed only a tiny fraction away from exact escape speed still reads as "parabolic (just escaping)", the same way a real launch never lands on exactly zero total energy in floating point either.
