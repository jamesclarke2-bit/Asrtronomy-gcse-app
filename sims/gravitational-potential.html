Gravitational Potential Energy and Orbital Energy

Why is gravitational potential energy always negative? Why does mgh stop working once you're far from a surface? And what actually happens to a satellite's energy as its orbit changes? All three come from the same engine — a point mass's gravitational field, g(r) = GM/r² — worked through honestly rather than quoted.

1. Who does the work?

Released from rest, nothing but gravity acts on it: the field's work all becomes kinetic energy, and potential energy falls by exactly that much — so the total, kinetic plus potential, never changes. It stays at the value it started with, for as long as the mass is actually in free fall.

This is the definition in practice: potential energy is defined as the work an external agent does bringing a mass in at constant speed, which is exactly why the "work done by the hand" row above and the "potential energy change" row above it show the same number — they're not two separate facts that happen to agree, they're the same fact stated twice.

Gravitational potential energy is defined as the work the hand does bringing the mass in from infinity at constant speed — which is exactly why it's negative.

Gravity pulls the mass inward on its own, every step of the way in. To keep the speed constant against that pull, the hand has to push back — outward, opposite to the direction the mass is actually moving — and a force applied opposite to motion is, by definition, negative work. So the hand's work (potential energy) comes out negative precisely because gravity's own work, over that same inward move, is positive. Flip the move around — haul the mass back out to infinity — and every sign flips too. That's also why zero potential sits at infinity: it's simply where the hand starts, before having done any work at all, and every step closer costs a bit more negative work.

Lowering all the way from the release point to the surface, the hand's total work approaches −GM/R as the release point moves out — and gets there faster than you might expect: 90% of the way there at 10 radii out, 99% at 100 radii. "Infinity" is not far away in practice.

4. mgh vs the exact formula

mgh assumes g stays constant over the climb — only true close to a surface. The exact energy gained climbing height h from radius R is ΔU = GMm(1/R − 1/(R+h)).

Exact: GMm(1/R − 1/(R+h))
Approximation: mgh
Ratio, exact ÷ mgh

Exact

mgh

At h = 50 m the two are indistinguishable to several decimal places; by the time h is a sizeable fraction of Earth's own radius (6,371 km), mgh is overestimating by a margin an exam-level calculation can't ignore — because g has measurably dropped over such a large climb, and mgh assumes it hasn't moved at all.

Moving up: kinetic energy falls (a higher orbit is a slower one) while potential energy rises faster than kinetic energy falls, so the total rises too (becomes less negative), even though the satellite is moving slower, not faster.

A real transfer between these orbits needs two engine burns, not one, and isn't shown here.

Where the line meets the curve is the highest point reached, for a bound (negative-energy) launch. At exactly zero total energy it just escapes, arriving at infinity with nothing left — this reads as the "parabolic" case even when a real speed lands only a tiny fraction away from the exact value, the same way a real launch never lands on exactly zero total energy in floating point either. Above zero it escapes with speed to spare.

The exact value is 11.19 km/s; anything above it escapes with speed to spare — including the slider's own nearest step, 11.2 km/s, which is already just past it.
