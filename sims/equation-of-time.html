The Equation of Time

Why a sundial and a clock rarely agree, and by how much across the year.

What the equation of time means

Equation of time = apparent solar time (AST) − mean solar time (MST). A positive value means the sundial is ahead of the clock; negative means it's behind.

Apparent Solar Time (AST)
What a sundial reads — it tracks the real Sun's actual position in the sky, exactly as seen.
Mean Solar Time (MST)
What a clock reads — it tracks an averaged, perfectly steady, fictitious "mean sun", invented purely so that clocks run at a constant rate all year round, unlike the real Sun.
Local Mean Time (LMT)
Mean solar time at one particular longitude. Mean noon arrives 4 minutes later for every degree further west, so before time zones every town kept its own slightly different LMT. See Local mean time, time zones and GMT below.

It comes from two combined effects: Earth's orbit is elliptical (it moves faster when nearer the Sun), and its axis is tilted relative to its orbit.

Two effects added together

The equation of time is the sum of two independent effects, both plotted below alongside the total:

  • Orbital eccentricity — Earth's orbit is an ellipse, not a circle, so Earth moves faster when closer to the Sun (near perihelion, early January) and slower when farther away (near aphelion, early July). This makes the apparent Sun run alternately ahead of and behind steady clock time, completing one full cycle per year.
  • Axial tilt — even on a perfectly circular orbit, the Sun's motion along the tilted ecliptic doesn't project evenly onto the celestial equator, which is what a clock effectively tracks. It runs fastest near the solstices and slowest near the equinoxes, completing two full cycles per year — so this effect is zero at every solstice and equinox.

Local mean time, time zones and GMT

Earth turns 360° in 24 hours: 15° every hour, or 1° every 4 minutes. So mean solar noon, when the mean sun crosses the meridian, reaches each place 4 minutes later for every degree of longitude further west. Mean solar time kept for one particular longitude is that place's Local Mean Time (LMT):

LMT = GMT + longitude ÷ 15 hours (east positive, west negative)

Until the mid-1800s, every town set its clocks to its own LMT. Bristol, 2.6° west of Greenwich, ran about 10 minutes behind London, and the clock on its Corn Exchange still has two minute hands to show both. Once railways let people travel between towns in hours, dozens of local times became unworkable. British railways adopted London time in the 1840s, it became the legal standard across Great Britain in 1880, and in 1884 an international conference chose Greenwich as longitude 0° for the whole world.

Today the world is divided into time zones. Each is roughly 15° wide, and everyone in it sets their clocks to the same standard time, usually a whole number of hours ahead of or behind GMT. Within a zone clocks agree, even though the true LMT varies across it (by about half an hour either side of the middle, or more where a country chooses one zone for a wide area: all of China uses GMT+8). Zone boundaries bend to follow national borders.

Greenwich Mean Time (GMT) is the mean solar time at Greenwich, on longitude 0°, and every zone is defined from it (Paris is GMT+1, New York GMT−5). Astronomers call it Universal Time (UT), and give observation times in UT so they can be compared anywhere in the world. For GCSE, GMT and UT are the same thing. (Everyday clocks now follow UTC, an atomic-clock version kept within a second of UT.) The UK uses GMT in winter and British Summer Time, GMT+1, in summer.

The readout box in the controls shows LMT and sundial time (AST = LMT + equation of time) when GMT is 12:00, for the date and longitude you choose. The time zone shown is just the nearest 15° meridian; real zones follow borders, and some countries add summer time.

Sunrise and sunset through the year

How long the Sun spends above the horizon depends on its declination, the same seasonal calculation that drives the analemma above and the Sun Path Simulator. From March to September the Sun is north of the celestial equator, so in the Northern Hemisphere its daily path is longer and higher, with early sunrises and late sunsets. From September to March it's south of the equator, and the days are short. The further from the equator you are, the bigger the swing.

The graph below shows sunrise and sunset across the whole year for the latitude you choose in the controls. Two things in it are worth a closer look:

  • The equinoxes aren't quite 12 hours of daylight. Sunrise and sunset are counted from the moment the Sun's top edge touches the horizon, and the atmosphere bends its light up over the horizon, so there's a few minutes more daylight than darkness even at an equinox.

Times are GMT at longitude 0°. At other longitudes they shift by 4 minutes per degree, and UK clocks show an hour later during British Summer Time, which isn't drawn so the curves stay smooth.
