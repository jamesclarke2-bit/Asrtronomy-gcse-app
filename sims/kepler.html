Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

Three rules that describe every orbit: the shape (an ellipse with the Sun at one focus), the changing speed around it (faster near the Sun, slower far away), and how period and distance relate across the whole Solar System. Drag the eccentricity slider, watch the planet move, and see the straight-line graph that falls out of the third law.

Every shaded wedge covers the same time interval, and every wedge has the same area — so the short, wide wedges near perihelion and the long, thin wedges near aphelion are swept out at the same rate. That's only possible if the planet moves fastest near perihelion and slowest near aphelion.

3. Third law: T² against r³

Every planet sits close to the same straight line through the origin — that line is Kepler's third law, T² = r³ (T in years, r in AU), for anything orbiting the Sun. Plotted points use the exam data sheet's own rounded distance and period figures, so a point can sit very slightly off the line — that's the data sheet's own rounding, not an error.

Orbit view and Second law both use a fixed 1 AU semi-major axis around our own Sun, so one full animated sweep always represents one real year, whatever the eccentricity is set to — period depends on the semi-major axis alone (Kepler's third law), never on eccentricity.
