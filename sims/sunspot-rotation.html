Sunspot Rotation

Sunspots are carried round the Sun by its own rotation — tracking one's position across the visible disc, day by day, is how real solar observers measure how fast the Sun actually spins.

Two ways to estimate the period from your readings

Method 1: double the limb-to-limb time

A spot crosses the whole visible disc — east limb to west limb — in exactly half a rotation (the far side, where it isn't visible, is the other half). Note the day it disappears off the west limb, then double that day count to estimate the full (synodic) rotation period.

Method 2: the two-reading "stretch" method

From any two readings, work out the angle the spot moved (its longitude on the second day minus its longitude on the first) and the days between them, then scale up to a full 360° rotation:

period = 360° × days ÷ angle moved

Recording a spot's position day by day like this is observation task A6 on the specification; using that data to calculate the rotation period, as both methods above do, is task B6.

Why the rotation period isn't one fixed number

Two separate effects both shift the number you measure away from a single "true" value.

The Sun isn't a solid body: differential rotation

A sunspot's own latitude changes how fast it's carried round. Using the differential rotation law ω = 14.71 − 2.40 sin²(φ) − 1.79 sin⁴(φ) degrees per day (sidereal), at latitude φ, gives sidereal periods of about 24.5 days at the equator, 25.7 days at 30°, and 30.2 days at 60°.

These three figures are a first draft, to be checked by the teacher against the notes' own simpler figures — about 25 days at the equator, about 35 days near the poles.

Earth moves too: sidereal vs synodic

The period you actually observe from Earth (the synodic period) is longer than the Sun's true rotation against the stars (the sidereal period), for the same reason the Moon's synodic month is longer than its sidereal one: Earth orbits the Sun in the same direction the Sun itself spins, so a sunspot has to turn a little further each rotation before it lines back up with Earth's new position. The relationship is 1/synodic = 1/sidereal − 1/365.25 (days in a year). A mean sidereal period of 25.38 days gives a synodic period of about 27.27 days.

Common exam confusion: the Sun's rotation period (~25–35 days, how fast it spins) and the solar cycle (~11 years, how sunspot number rises and falls) are entirely different things, on wildly different timescales.

Observing safely

Never look directly at the Sun. The safe way to track a real sunspot is to project its image — through a simple pinhole, or more sharply through a small telescope.
